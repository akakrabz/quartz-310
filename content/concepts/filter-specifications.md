> [!abstract] In one breath
> - A practical filter only approximates the ideal box. Its passband wobbles (**passband ripple**), its stopband is small but not zero (**stopband attenuation**), and between them lies a **transition band** that is neither passed nor stopped.
> - Read all three on a dB plot of the magnitude, $20\log_{10}\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert$: $-20$ dB is a gain of $\frac1{10}$, $-40$ dB is $\frac1{100}$.
> - The tradeoff this course cares about: for a given filter length, a narrower transition band costs stopband attenuation, and the other way round.

> [!key] Words for a practical magnitude response (Lecture 27 §2, slide 7)
> - **Passband:** a range of frequencies the filter lets through. (A band-stop filter has two.)
> - **Passband ripple:** how much the gain varies inside the passband, measured about its centre. Ideal: 0. The dashed pair of lines in the figure is twice the ripple, top to bottom. Sometimes written $\delta_p$.
> - **Stopband:** a range of frequencies the filter attenuates. (A band-pass filter has two.)
> - **Stopband attenuation:** the **highest** gain reached anywhere in the stopband, the worst case of how much unwanted frequencies are reduced. Ideal: gain 0, which is $-\infty$ dB.
> - **Transition band:** the range between a passband edge and the neighbouring stopband edge. The ideal filters have none.
> - **Transition bandwidth:** the width of that range, $\lvert\omega_p-\omega_s\rvert$, in rad/sample. Ideal: 0.
>
> The plot is in decibels, $\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert_{dB}=20\log_{10}\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert$:
>
> | dB | 0 | $-6$ | $-20$ | $-23$ | $-40$ | $-100$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | gain | 1 | about $\frac12$ | $\frac1{10}$ | 0.071 | $\frac1{100}$ | $10^{-5}$ |
>
> The notes' Figure 2 has a stopband attenuation of about $-23$ dB (unwanted frequencies come through at about 7% of their amplitude, "roughly $\frac1{10}$") and a transition bandwidth of $\frac\pi2-\frac{3\pi}{8}=\frac\pi8$.

## 2. What we want, and what it costs

We want small passband ripple, strong stopband attenuation and a narrow transition band, with a filter that is short and cheap. They cannot all be had at once.

- **Why ripple is bad:** two frequencies that should both pass unchanged come out with different gains. His example: one amplified by about 20%, the other attenuated by about 20% ($+1.6$ dB and $-1.9$ dB).
- **Why a wide transition band is bad:** frequencies inside it are "neither properly passing … through, nor … properly attenuating or stopping them fully."
- **The tradeoff in ECE 310:** transition bandwidth against stopband attenuation. "If I want to make one better, it's almost certainly going to make the other worse." It is the main-lobe width against side-lobe height tradeoff of [[concepts/window-functions|window functions]] in a new setting: the rectangular window has the narrow lobe and $-13$ dB side lobes, the Hamming window twice the width and $-42$ dB.
- **Passband ripple** is not pursued further in this course ("we don't talk about passband ripple really much in this class"); ECE 311 looks at it by experiment.

The filter in the figure above is the simplest practical design, the ideal sinc shifted and cut to 17 samples. Its numbers: ripple 0.088, stopband attenuation $-20.4$ dB, transition bandwidth about $0.10\pi$. Not good, and Lectures 28–29 are about doing better.

## 3. From a scenario to a specification

> [!recipe] Which filter, and where are its edges? (Lecture 27, Example 1)
> 1. **Decide what to keep and what to remove.** Remove low, keep high → high-pass. Remove high, keep low → low-pass. Keep a middle band → band-pass. Remove a middle band → band-stop.
> 2. **Convert every frequency to rad/sample:** $\omega=\Omega T=2\pi f\,T=\frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$. Everything must lie between 0 and $\pi$ (below $\frac{f_s}{2}$).
> 3. **Place the edges on the content.** The passband edge is the edge of what you keep; the stopband edge is the edge of what you remove. The gap between them is the transition band. "Largest possible transition band" means: use the whole gap.
> 4. **State the bands** for $0\le\omega\le\pi$, and the transition bandwidth as the difference of the two edges.

> [!question] Example 1(a) (slide 8, worked in lecture) — thunder
> Audio is recorded at 4 kHz during a thunderstorm. The thunder has a maximum frequency of 200 Hz; the voices have a lowest pitch of 300 Hz. Specify the filter that removes the thunder, with the largest possible transition band.

> [!success]- Answer
> Remove low frequencies, keep higher ones: a **high-pass** filter. $T=\frac{1}{4000}$ s.
> $$
> \omega_s=2\pi\cdot200\cdot T=\frac{400\pi}{4000}=\frac{\pi}{10},\qquad\omega_p=2\pi\cdot300\cdot T=\frac{600\pi}{4000}=\frac{3\pi}{20}.
> $$
> Stopband $\left[0,\frac{\pi}{10}\right]$, passband $\left[\frac{3\pi}{20},\pi\right]$, transition band between them with bandwidth $\lvert\omega_p-\omega_s\rvert=\frac{3\pi}{20}-\frac{2\pi}{20}=\frac{\pi}{20}$ rad/sample.

> [!question] Example 1(b) (slide 8, left as an exercise) — mains hum
> A microphone system runs at $f_s=480$ Hz. Electrical interference adds a strong hum at 60 Hz. Specify the filter that removes the hum while allowing a transition bandwidth of $\frac{\pi}{12}$.

> [!success]- Answer
> Remove one frequency in the middle, keep everything on both sides: a **band-stop** filter (a notch). The hum sits at
> $$
> \omega_0=\frac{2\pi\cdot60}{480}=\frac{\pi}{4}.
> $$
> The stopband only has to contain $\omega_0$, so put both stopband edges at $\frac\pi4$ and allow a transition band of width $\frac{\pi}{12}$ on each side:
> $$
> \text{passbands }\left[0,\ \tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac{\pi}{12}\right]=\left[0,\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right]\ \text{and}\ \left[\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac{\pi}{12},\ \pi\right]=\left[\tfrac{\pi}{3},\pi\right],\qquad\text{stopband at }\omega=\tfrac{\pi}{4}.
> $$
> In hertz the passbands are 0–40 Hz and 80–240 Hz.
>
> Worked here from the definitions. In lecture he said he would upload the solution to part (b); it was not among the course files when this page was written, so compare with it when it appears.

> [!trap]
> - **Strong, not "high".** Strong stopband attenuation = a very negative number of dB = a tiny gain.
> - **20, not 10:** magnitudes use $20\log_{10}$. A factor of 10 in amplitude is 20 dB.
> - Transition bandwidth is a difference of two edge frequencies in rad/sample, not a ratio and not in hertz unless asked.
> - For the largest transition band, the passband and stopband edges sit exactly on the content edges. Putting $\omega_p=\omega_s$ asks for an ideal filter.
> - The stopband attenuation is the worst (highest) point of the stopband, not its average.
