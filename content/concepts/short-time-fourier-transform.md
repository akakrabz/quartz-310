> [!abstract] In one breath
> - One DFT of a whole recording tells you **which** frequencies it contains, not **when**: the order of the notes is gone.
> - STFT: slide a length-$L$ window along the signal in steps of $S$ samples and take a length-$L$ DFT of every piece. The result $X[t,k]$ has a time index $t$ and a frequency index $k$.
> - A long window separates close frequencies but blurs time; a short window does the opposite. A spectrogram is $\lvert X[t,k]\rvert$ in dB, drawn as an image.

## 1. Why one DFT is not enough

Lectures 22–23 analysed $x[n]=\sum_{m=1}^{M}A_m\cos(\omega_mn)$, $0\le n\le N-1$ (eq. 1). That model assumes every component starts and stops together. Music does not: a scale is eight notes played one after the other. The DFT of the whole recording shows the eight peaks (and their overtones), but it would show the same peaks whatever the order of the notes, because "the DFT takes us completely out of the time-domain" (notes §1).

> [!lecture] In lecture — L24, Oct 28
> "This problem statement has actually a really crucial issue with it": we do not have to mandate that the components start at the same time. With notes played over time "these cosines are starting and stopping." Looking at one DFT of a C major scale, "I have no idea what order in which notes were played … just by taking the DFT of that full signal, I remove all temporal information." The fix: "take that piece of audio and chop it up into windows."

## 2. The definition (§1)

> [!key] Short-time Fourier transform (Lecture 24, eqs. 2–3)
> $x[n]$ has length $N$; $w[m]$ is a window of length $L\lt N$ (rectangular, Hamming, …); the step size or **stride** is $S$, $1\le S\le L$:
> $$
> X[t,k]=\sum_{m=0}^{L-1}x[St+m]\,w[m]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi k}{L}m},\qquad 0\le k\le L-1,\quad 0\le t\le\frac{N-L}{S}-1.
> $$
> - Column $t$ is the length-$L$ DFT of the windowed piece that starts at sample $St$. Bin $k$ means $\omega_k=\frac{2\pi k}{L}$, or $f_k=\frac{kf_s}{L}$ Hz, exactly as for any [[concepts/dft|DFT]].
> - $S=1$ (eq. 2) takes a new DFT at every sample: about $N-L$ DFTs, and neighbours share all but one sample, so they are nearly identical and the work is wasted.
> - $S=L$: the windows do not overlap. $L=S=N$: a single DFT, no time information at all.
> - The notes print the range of eq. 3 as $0\le k\le N$; each DFT has length $L$, so $k$ stops at $L-1$.

> [!lecture] In lecture — L24, Oct 28
> Moving one sample at a time, "the two DFTs should be virtually the same because all but one sample are in common, so this is really wasteful." In practice "we pick a step size … really common choices are something like half of the window length or a third of the window length. You want to have some amount of overlap, but you don't want to overlap too much between windows because then it becomes really expensive and redundant."

## 3. The tradeoff: time against frequency (§1.1)

> [!key] What $L$ and $S$ buy (Lecture 24 §1.1, slide 10)
> With sampling rate $f_s$:
> $$
> \text{time covered by one window}=\frac{L}{f_s},\qquad\text{time between windows}=\frac{S}{f_s}.
> $$
> - **Frequency resolution** depends on $L$ only. Every component shows up as the window's main lobe, of width $\frac{4\pi}{L}$ (rectangular) or $\frac{8\pi}{L}$ (Hamming), as in [[concepts/window-functions|Lecture 23]]. In hertz that is $\frac{2f_s}{L}$ or $\frac{4f_s}{L}$.
> - **Time resolution** gets worse as $L$ grows: a window that covers half a second cannot say where inside that half second something happened.
> - **Cost:** a smaller $S$ means more DFTs; a larger $L$ means each DFT is more expensive.
>
> | choice | gain | price |
> |---|---|---|
> | smaller $L$ | precise in time; cheap DFTs | wide main lobes, fewer frequencies evaluated |
> | larger $L$ | narrow main lobes | blurred in time; expensive DFTs |
> | smaller $S$ | changes are caught quickly | more DFTs to compute |
> | larger $S$ | fewer DFTs | the spectrum may change a lot between two windows |

> [!question] Notes example (§1.1) — choosing $L$ for a piano scale
> Each note of a scale lasts 0.5 s and the audio is recorded at 10 kHz. We want ten windows per note. (a) What window length $L$? (b) Not in the notes: with a rectangular window, how far apart must two notes be for their main lobes to separate fully? Is that enough for C4 (261.6 Hz) and D4 (293.7 Hz)?

> [!success]- Answer
> (a) Ten windows in 0.5 s: each covers 0.05 s, so $L=0.05\times10\,000=500$ samples.
>
> (b) Full-lobe separation ([[concepts/frequency-resolution|frequency resolution]]) needs $L\gt\frac{4\pi}{\Delta\omega}$, that is $\Delta f\gt\frac{2f_s}{L}=\frac{20\,000}{500}=40$ Hz. C4 and D4 are $32$ Hz apart: with $L=500$ their lobes still overlap (they pass the weaker half-lobe criterion, $\frac{f_s}{L}=20$ Hz). Full separation needs $L\gt\frac{2f_s}{\Delta f}=\frac{20\,000}{32.03}\approx624$ samples, 62.5 ms, which leaves 8 windows per note instead of 10. Better frequency resolution was paid for with time resolution.

## 4. Reading a spectrogram (§2)

A **spectrogram** plots the STFT as an image: time on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical axis, and the brightness of each point is the magnitude $\lvert X[t,k]\rvert$, usually in dB. The notes' two figures show what to look for:

- **A scale** (Figure 1: C major on guitar, $f_s=48$ kHz, $L=2^{12}=4096$, $S=\frac L3\approx1365$): one horizontal bar per note, each about 0.5 s long, stepping upward like a staircase. Each window covers $\frac{4096}{48\,000}\approx0.085$ s and consecutive windows overlap by about 0.057 s.
- **Overtones:** fainter bars at integer multiples of each note's frequency (a note near 262 Hz also shows at about 523 and 785 Hz). They are the other resonances of the string.
- **Low-frequency smudges** at the start of each note: the pick hitting the string.
- **A chord** (Figure 2: a four-chord loop, each chord played twice): several bars at the same time, repeating twice before the pattern changes.

> [!lecture] In lecture — L24, Oct 28
> Zooming into the chord: "the lower frequencies down here occur just before the higher frequencies. What that actually tells me is that the guitar was strummed downward instead of upward," because a downward strum hits the thicker, lower strings first: "something that is really not accessible in the time" domain. And on the status of all this: "this is just for fun. You're not being tested on it, just because it's kind of hard to test you on it, because it's a very computational thing."

> [!trap]
> - A longer window does not give "a better spectrogram", only a different compromise. There is no choice of $L$ that is sharp in both directions.
> - Zero-padding each window makes the picture smoother along the frequency axis but does not narrow the main lobes ([[concepts/zero-padding|zero padding]]).
> - A small stride $S$ adds columns, not resolution: the time blur is set by $L$.
