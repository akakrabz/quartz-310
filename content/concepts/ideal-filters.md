> [!abstract] In one breath
> - Four canonical frequency-selective filters: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, band-stop. **Ideal** means gain exactly 1 in the passband, exactly 0 in the stopband, and nothing in between.
> - The ideal low-pass filter has $h[n]=\frac{\sin(\omega_cn)}{\pi n}$: infinitely long, non-causal and not absolutely summable. It cannot be built.
> - The other three are made from a low-pass: $h_{hpf}[n]=\delta[n]-h_{lpf}[n]$, a band-pass by multiplying with a cosine, a band-stop as $1-$ band-pass. Learn to design a low-pass and the rest follow.

## 1. The four canonical types (§1.1)

A **frequency-selective** filter is an LTI system chosen for its [[concepts/frequency-response|frequency response]]: it lets some bands of frequency through unchanged and removes others. The classic use is de-noising: the signal lives in one band, the noise in another.

- **Passband:** the frequencies the filter passes with no distortion.
- **Stopband:** the frequencies it suppresses as strongly as possible.

> [!key] Ideal responses (Lecture 27, eqs. 1–4)
> For $-\pi\le\omega\le\pi$ (every response repeats with period $2\pi$):
> $$
> H_{lpf}(\omega)=\begin{cases}1,&\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_c\\0,&\omega_c\lt\lvert\omega\rvert\le\pi\end{cases}\qquad
> H_{hpf}(\omega)=\begin{cases}0,&\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_c\\1,&\omega_c\lt\lvert\omega\rvert\le\pi\end{cases}
> $$
> $$
> H_{bpf}(\omega)=\begin{cases}1,&\omega_l\le\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_u\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad
> H_{bsf}(\omega)=\begin{cases}0,&\omega_l\le\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_u\\1,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
> $$
> - $\omega_c$ is the **cutoff frequency**; $\omega_l$ and $\omega_u$ are the lower and upper cutoffs of a band.
> - Low-pass keeps the slowly varying part of a signal; high-pass keeps the fast part. "High" means near $\omega=\pm\pi$, the highest frequency a sequence can have.
> - Band-pass: one passband, two stopbands. Band-stop: two passbands, one stopband.
> - All frequencies are digital, in rad/sample. A frequency in hertz converts with $\omega=\Omega T=\frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$ ([[concepts/sampling|sampling]]).

## 2. The ideal low-pass impulse response (§1.2, slide 5)

> [!key] Ideal low-pass filter (Lecture 27, eq. 5)
> $$
> h_{lpf}[n]=\frac{\sin(\omega_cn)}{\pi n}=\frac{\omega_c}{\pi}\,\mathrm{sinc}(\omega_cn),\qquad-\infty\lt n\lt\infty,\qquad h_{lpf}[0]=\frac{\omega_c}{\pi}.
> $$
> With $\mathrm{sinc}(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t}$, the cutoff can be read straight from the argument of the sinc. This is the "ideal low-pass" entry of the [[concepts/dtft-pairs|DTFT pairs]], and the same sinc that interpolates in [[concepts/ideal-reconstruction|ideal D/A conversion]].

> [!derivation]- Inverse DTFT of the box (slide 5)
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> h[n]&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H_{lpf}(\omega)\,e^{j\omega n}\,d\omega=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}1\cdot e^{j\omega n}\,d\omega\\
> &=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left.\frac{e^{j\omega n}}{jn}\right|_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}=\frac{1}{\pi n}\cdot\frac{e^{j\omega_cn}-e^{-j\omega_cn}}{2j}=\frac{\sin(\omega_cn)}{\pi n}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The response is 1 only between $\pm\omega_c$, which shrinks the limits of the integral; the last step is Euler's formula for the sine. Multiplying and dividing by $\omega_c$ gives the sinc form. For $n=0$ integrate directly: $\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot2\omega_c=\frac{\omega_c}{\pi}$.

## 3. Three reasons it cannot be built

> [!key] Issues with the ideal filter (Lecture 27 §1.2)
> 1. **Infinitely long, with no difference equation.** The convolution sum would never end. Infinite length alone is not fatal: $y[n]=\frac12y[n-1]+x[n]$ has the infinite $h[n]=\left(\frac12\right)^nu[n]$ and is easy to run. But no LCCDE, with or without feedback, produces the sinc.
> 2. **Non-causal.** $h[n]\ne0$ for $n\lt0$, so each output needs future inputs. That is acceptable for stored data such as an image, and impossible in real time.
> 3. **Not BIBO stable.** $\sum_n\lvert h[n]\rvert=\infty$, because the sinc decays only like $\frac1n$ ([[concepts/bibo-stability|BIBO stability]]). This one is more theoretical: as soon as the response is cut to a finite length it is FIR and therefore stable.
>
> The same three issues apply to the ideal high-pass, band-pass and band-stop filters, since each is built from a low-pass (§4).
>
> **The practical way out** (coming lectures): shift the sinc and truncate it. That gives a causal, finite filter with linear phase, but only an approximation of the box, with the imperfections named in [[concepts/filter-specifications|filter specifications]].

Why no difference equation can do it: an LCCDE has a rational $H(z)$, a ratio of two polynomials, and a non-zero rational function has only finitely many zeros. Its frequency response can touch zero at isolated frequencies but cannot stay at zero over a whole band.

> [!lecture] In lecture — L27, Nov 9
> "There are actually three issues with this impulse response. Two of them are pretty significant. The third one is actually technically a result of one of them." A student: the sinc is not finite length. Yes, and more than that, "there is no actual proper linear constant coefficient difference equation that can implement this impulse response." Non-causal: "this microphone system that I'm talking into … cannot work non-causally. My voice is coming in in real time." The instability he wrote in square brackets with an asterisk: make the filter finite and "it'll implicitly make the system stable."

## 4. The other three from a low-pass

> [!key] One low-pass design gives all four (Lecture 27 §1.2, slide 3)
> - **High-pass:** $H_{hpf}(\omega)=1-H_{lpf}(\omega)$. The inverse DTFT of the constant 1 is $\delta[n]$, so
> $$
> h_{hpf}[n]=\delta[n]-h_{lpf}[n]\qquad\text{(same cutoff }\omega_c\text{)}.
> $$
> - **Band-pass:** modulate a low-pass, $h_{bpf}[n]=\cos(\omega_0n)\,h_{lpf}[n]$. By the modulation property ([[concepts/dtft-properties|DTFT properties]]) the response is $\frac12H_{lpf}(\omega-\omega_0)+\frac12H_{lpf}(\omega+\omega_0)$: the box moves to $\pm\omega_0$, so the passband is $\omega_0-\omega_c\le\lvert\omega\rvert\le\omega_0+\omega_c$.
> - **Band-stop:** $H_{bsf}(\omega)=1-H_{bpf}(\omega)$, that is $\delta[n]-h_{bpf}[n]$.
>
> A detail the notes leave out: the cosine halves the gain, so the band-pass above has height $\frac12$. Use $2\cos(\omega_0n)\,h_{lpf}[n]$ for a passband of height 1, and take the band-stop from that version.

> [!question] Asked in lecture (Nov 9) — why not just zero the DFT bins?
> To low-pass a recorded signal, why not take its DFT, set every bin above the cutoff to zero, and take the inverse DFT?

> [!success]- Answer
> Setting bins to zero multiplies $X[k]$ by a mask of ones and zeros. Multiplication in the DFT domain is [[concepts/circular-convolution|circular convolution]] in time, with the inverse DFT of the mask, a sequence as long as the signal itself. Nothing was zero-padded, so the result wraps around: the end of the signal leaks into its beginning, the artifact that [[concepts/fast-convolution|fast convolution]] avoids by padding. Padding first does not rescue the idea: a mask of length $M$ has an inverse DFT of length $M$, and a length-$N$ signal convolved with it would need $N+M-1\gt M$ samples. And between the bins the response is not a clean 0 or 1 at all.
>
> In his words: "I can tell you from experience when I was a 310 student that I tried this. It does not work." Masking produces aliasing in time, "not aliasing in the Nyquist sampling sense, but … parts of the end and front of the signal are going to interact with each other in a really strange way." The related design method, frequency sampling, "leads to a very large passband ripple … because we're setting these frequency targets, but then what happens in between those frequencies is really very often quite wobbly."

> [!trap]
> - $1-H_{lpf}(\omega)$ is a statement about frequency responses. In time it is $\delta[n]-h_{lpf}[n]$, not $1-h_{lpf}[n]$.
> - Cutoffs are digital frequencies between 0 and $\pi$. Convert hertz with $\omega=\frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$ before naming a band.
> - "High-pass" does not mean large $\lvert\omega\rvert$ without limit: responses repeat every $2\pi$, and the highest frequency is $\pi$.
> - The ideal filter is a reference to measure against, not a design target that more effort will reach.
> - A cosine modulation halves the gain.
