> [!key] Definition (Lecture 16, notes Eq. 30)
> $$
> \tau_{gd}(\omega)=-\frac{d\angle H_d(\omega)}{d\omega}\qquad\text{(samples)}
> $$
> It "expresses how an LTI system will delay each frequency component in a signal" (slide 12). Take the derivative of the **continuous** phase: the $\pm\pi$ jumps and $2\pi$ wraps of the plotted principal phase are removed first, or simply skipped, since between them the slope is well defined (Singer–Munson §6.4: the phase "with jumps of $\pi$ and $2\pi$ removed").
> - **Linear phase** $\angle H_d(\omega)=\alpha-k\omega$, jumps allowed $\Rightarrow$ $\tau_{gd}(\omega)=k$: **uniform group delay**. Every frequency is delayed by $k$ samples and the waveform keeps its shape.
> - Any other phase $\Rightarrow$ $\tau_{gd}$ depends on $\omega$: **non-uniform group delay**. Different frequencies are delayed by different amounts and the waveform is distorted.

**Why "delay".** The DTFT delay property $x[n-k]\leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega k}X_d(\omega)$ says that a shift by $k$ samples adds the phase $-k\omega$, a line of slope $-k$. So the pure delay $H_d(\omega)=e^{-jk\omega}$ sends $e^{j\omega_0n}$ to $e^{j\omega_0(n-k)}$ for every $\omega_0$, i.e. $y[n]=x[n-k]$ ([[3-fourier-analysis/16-magnitude-and-phase-response|Lecture 16]] §6). Group delay reads the slope of *any* phase as a delay, frequency by frequency.

| system | phase (continuous part) | $\tau_{gd}(\omega)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $e^{-jk\omega}$, $h=\delta[n-k]$ | $-k\omega$ | $k$ |
| $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},0,1\}$, $H_d=2e^{-j\omega}\cos\omega$ | $-\omega$ ($\pi$ jumps at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$) | $1$ |
| $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},0,-1\}$, $H_d=2je^{-j\omega}\sin\omega$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega$ ($\pi$ jump at $0$) | $1$ |
| $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{2},0,0,2\}$, $H_d=4e^{-j\frac32\omega}\cos\frac{3\omega}{2}$ | $-\frac32\omega$ ($\pi$ jumps at $\pm\frac{\pi}{3}$) | $\frac32$ |
| $y[n]=x[n]+2x[n-3]+x[n-6]$ ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025\|SP2025 MT2]] #3) | $-3\omega$ | $3$ |
| $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{3},2,1\}$ (Lecture 16, Example 3) | $-\tan^{-1}\frac{2\sin\omega+\sin2\omega}{3+2\cos\omega+\cos2\omega}$ | $\frac{3c(1+c)}{3c^2+4c+2}$, $c=\cos\omega$: $\frac23$ at $0$, $0$ at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pm\pi$, down to $-\frac{3\sqrt2}{4}\approx-1.06$ near $\pm0.70\pi$ |

(All checked against `scipy.signal.group_delay`.) A half-sample group delay such as $\frac32$ is perfectly meaningful even though $h$ has no sample at $n=\frac32$, and negative values (Example 3 at high frequencies) are allowed for a causal filter: they only say the phase rises with $\omega$ there. One handy check: at $\omega=0$, the group delay of a real FIR filter is the "centre of mass" $\sum_n n\,h[n]\big/\sum_n h[n]$ of its taps (for $\{3,2,1\}$: $\frac{0+2+2}{6}=\frac23$).

## Same magnitude, different phase

The lecture's illustration (notes Fig. 3, slides 13–15) filters a noisy photo with two systems that share one low-pass magnitude response and differ only in phase. The linear-phase system returns a clean image, shifted by a few pixels. The system whose phase oscillates sinusoidally in $\omega$ returns the same frequencies with the same strengths, but its output shakes with ghost edges and ringing, because its group delay swings between large positive and negative values. > [!intuition]- Why a sinusoidal phase makes echoes (beyond the lectures)
> A phase ripple $a\sin(M\omega)$ is a sum of pure delays. The Jacobi–Anger expansion (Bessel functions, not part of ECE 310) says
> $$
> e^{ja\sin(M\omega)}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}J_k(a)\,e^{jkM\omega},
> $$
> and each $e^{jkM\omega}$ is a shift by $kM$ samples. So the sinusoidal-phase output is the linear-phase output plus copies shifted by multiples of $M$, weighted by $J_k(a)$. For $a=2.6$: $J_0\approx-0.10$, $J_{\pm1}\approx\pm0.47$, $J_{\pm2}\approx0.46$, $J_{\pm3}\approx\pm0.24$. Almost none of the energy stays in the unshifted copy, which is why the image in the notes is covered in ghost copies rather than just blurred. (Checked: the figure's output equals that Bessel-weighted sum of shifted copies to $10^{-10}$.)

## Which filters have linear phase? (beyond Lecture 16)

The lecture's linear-phase examples, $\{1,0,1\}$ and $\{1,0,-1\}$, are symmetric and antisymmetric about their centre, and the non-linear one, $\{3,2,1\}$, is not. That is the general rule, stated in the course's Singer–Munson notes (§6.4, "generalized linear phase filters", ahead of filter design later in the course):

> [!key] Symmetric or antisymmetric FIR ⇒ (generalized) linear phase
> Let $h[n]$ be real with length $N$ (samples $n=0,\dots,N-1$), and let $M=\frac{N-1}{2}$ be its centre.
> - **Symmetric**, $h[n]=h[N-1-n]$: $H_d(\omega)=e^{-jM\omega}R(\omega)$ with $R$ real.
> - **Antisymmetric**, $h[n]=-h[N-1-n]$: $H_d(\omega)=je^{-jM\omega}R(\omega)$ with $R$ real.
>
> Either way the phase is $-M\omega$ (plus $\frac{\pi}{2}$ in the antisymmetric case) plus $\pm\pi$ jumps where $R$ changes sign, so $\tau_{gd}=M=\frac{N-1}{2}$ at every frequency. Singer and Munson call this **generalized** linear phase, because $R(\omega)$ may be negative; strictly linear phase would need $H_d=|H_d|e^{-jM\omega}$, with no jumps at all.

Why: pair each tap with its mirror image about the centre. For a symmetric $h$,
$$
h[n]e^{-j\omega n}+h[N-1-n]e^{-j\omega(N-1-n)}=h[n]e^{-jM\omega}\big(e^{-j\omega(n-M)}+e^{j\omega(n-M)}\big)=2h[n]\cos\big(\omega(n-M)\big)\,e^{-jM\omega},
$$
a real number times $e^{-jM\omega}$; for an antisymmetric $h$ the bracket becomes $-2j\sin(\omega(n-M))$. That is exactly the Lecture 16 trick, done for every pair at once. Singer–Munson also note that an IIR filter with a rational $H(z)$ cannot have generalized linear phase, which is one reason FIR filters are used when the phase matters. (The rule was checked numerically on random symmetric and antisymmetric filters of lengths 5–8.)

> [!trap]
> - **Jumps do not make a phase non-linear.** A sawtooth phase plot (2π wraps) or a line broken by π jumps is still linear phase; $\{1,0,1\}$ has $\tau_{gd}=1$ everywhere. Read the slope between the breaks.
> - **The value of the phase is not the delay; the slope is.** For $e^{-j2\omega}$, the principal angle at $\omega=\frac{3\pi}{4}$ is $+\frac{\pi}{2}$, but the system delays every frequency by $2$.
> - **A non-integer slope is not a shifted sample.** $X_d(\omega)=e^{-j\omega/2}$ on $|\omega|\le\pi$ is not "$\delta[n-\frac12]$" (that is not a sequence): its inverse DTFT is $x[n]=\frac{\sin(\pi(n-\frac12))}{\pi(n-\frac12)}$, nonzero at every $n$ ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] T/F (b), False; [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2019|FA2019 MT2]] #5 asks for the inverse DTFT of $e^{-j\omega/3}$, which the key writes as $\operatorname{sinc}(\pi(n-\frac13))$ with $\operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin x}{x}$; [[homework/hw6|HW6]] #3(b) has $e^{-j3.5\omega}$). The group delay of $e^{-j\omega\beta}$ is still $\beta$: the output is the band-limited interpolation of the input, delayed by $\beta$ samples. See [[concepts/dtft-pairs|DTFT pairs]].
> - **Zero phase is linear phase with slope $0$.** $h=\delta[n+2]+\delta[n]+\delta[n-2]$ has the real response $1+2\cos2\omega$ ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #3): $\tau_{gd}=0$, but the filter is non-causal. Delaying it by $2$ samples makes it causal and gives $\tau_{gd}=2$.
