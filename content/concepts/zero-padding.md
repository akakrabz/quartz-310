*Lectures 20 (Fri Oct 16) §2.3 and 22 (Wed Oct 21) §2.1 · family: [[problems/dft-values-and-zero-padding|DFT values and zero padding]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> - Append $L$ zeros: same nonzero samples, same DTFT; the $(N+L)$-point DFT samples $X_d(\omega)$ every $\frac{2\pi}{N+L}$.
> - $\tilde X[k']=X[k]$ exactly when $\frac{k'}{N+L}=\frac kN$.
> - A denser view of the same spectrum: no new information, no narrower lobes, no better [[concepts/frequency-resolution|resolution]].

## 1. Definition

> [!key] Zero padding (Lecture 20, eqs. 20–25)
> Append $L$ zeros:
> $$
> \tilde x[n]=\begin{cases}x[n], & 0\le n\le N-1\\ 0, & N\le n\le N+L-1\end{cases}
> $$
> Same nonzero samples, so the same DTFT, $\tilde X_d(\omega)=X_d(\omega)$, and
> $$
> \tilde X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j\frac{2\pi k}{N+L}n}=X_d\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N+L}\right),\qquad0\le k\le N+L-1 .
> $$
> Spacing $\frac{2\pi}{N+L}$ instead of $\frac{2\pi}{N}$: a denser view of the same spectrum, for the cost of a longer DFT.

> [!recipe] Which padded bin equals $X[k]$?
> Equate frequencies, $\frac{2\pi k}{N}=\frac{2\pi k'}{N+L}$: $k'=\frac{(N+L)\,k}{N}$, a match only when it is an integer. Padding to a multiple $mN$: $\tilde X[mk]=X[k]$.

## 2. Exercises

> [!question] Notes Exercise 1
> $x[n]$ has length 16 and DFT $X[k]$. Zero-pad it with 48 zeros to the length-64 $\tilde x[n]$ with DFT $\tilde X[k]$. Which values of $\tilde X[k]$ equal (a) $X[1]$, (b) $X[9]$?

> [!success]- Answer (eqs. 26–31)
> (a) $X[1]$ sits at $\frac{2\pi\cdot1}{16}=\frac{\pi}{8}$; $\frac{2\pi k}{64}=\frac{\pi}{8}\Rightarrow k=4$: $\boldsymbol{X[1]=\tilde X[4]}$.
>
> (b) $\frac{2\pi k}{64}=\frac{2\pi\cdot9}{16}\Rightarrow k=36$: $\boldsymbol{X[9]=\tilde X[36]}$.

> [!question] Slides — Zero-padding Example 1
> $x[n]$ has length 16 and DFT $X[k]$. Zero-pad it with 32 zeros to $y[n]$ with DFT $Y[k]$. Which values of $Y[k]$ equal (a) $X[0]$, (b) $X[1]$, (c) $X[4]$?

> [!success]- Answer
> Length $48=3\cdot16$, so $\frac{2\pi k}{16}=\frac{2\pi\cdot3k}{48}$: $\boldsymbol{X[0]=Y[0]}$, $\boldsymbol{X[1]=Y[3]}$, $\boldsymbol{X[4]=Y[12]}$.

> [!question] Slides — Zero-padding Example 2
> A length-$N$ signal $x[n]\overset{\mathcal F}{\longleftrightarrow}X_d(\omega)$. We want the DFT $X[k]$ to contain $X_d\!\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)$ and $X_d\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$. What are the two smallest possible values of $N$?

> [!success]- Answer
> Need integers $k_1$, $k_2$ with $\frac{2\pi k_1}{N}=\frac{\pi}{5}$ and $\frac{2\pi k_2}{N}=\frac{\pi}{3}$: $N=10k_1=6k_2$, a multiple of $\mathrm{lcm}(10,6)=30$. Two smallest: $\boldsymbol{N=30}$ ($k_1=3$, $k_2=5$) and $\boldsymbol{N=60}$ ($k_1=6$, $k_2=10$). Zero-pad $x[n]$ to reach them.

## 3. Zero padding for spectral analysis (Lecture 22 §2.1)

- Length-$N$ $s[n]$ with $L$ zeros appended: $S[k]$ has spacing $\frac{2\pi}{N}$, $\tilde S[k]$ spacing $\frac{2\pi}{N+L}\lt\frac{2\pi}{N}$; the underlying DTFTs are equal. A denser visualization that can make the sinusoidal components clearer.
- The notes' example ([[concepts/spectral-analysis|spectral analysis]] §2, Figure 3): two tones at $0.55\pi$ and $0.65\pi$, $N=32$, show one peak; padded with $96$ zeros to $128$, two main lobes at $\approx0.547\pi$ and $\approx0.641\pi$, the second half as tall.
- Zeros add no energy and no information: padding only reads more values of the same DTFT.
- It does **not** help [[concepts/frequency-resolution|lobe separation]] (Lecture 23 §2): the lobes stay $\frac{4\pi}{N}$ wide; resolving closer tones needs more real samples.
- Padding for [[concepts/circular-convolution|circular convolution]]: both inputs to $N\ge N_1+N_2-1$ makes circular equal linear.

> [!trap]
> - **Zero padding never changes $X_d(\omega)$;** it only refines the DFT grid.
> - **Padded bin $\frac{(N+L)k}{N}$, not $k$:** in general $X[1]\ne\tilde X[1]$.
> - **$\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{2\pi}{6}$ needs $N$ a multiple of 6,** not 3: $N=15$ gives $k=2.5$.
> - Denser is not sharper: a padded peak is still a $\frac{4\pi}{N}$ lobe.
> - $X_d(\omega)=Y_d(3\omega)$ after padding to $3N$ is false; $X_d(\omega)=Y_d(\omega)$ and $X[k]=Y[3k]$ are true.
