*Lecture 18 · Mon Oct 12, 2026 · notes + slides "Ideal digital-to-analog conversion" · prev: [[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/17-ideal-analog-to-digital-conversion|Lecture 17]] · next: [[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/19-aliasing|Lecture 19]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> To turn a sequence back into a continuous-time signal, put each sample back at its instant $t=nT$ and fill in between with one pulse per sample: $x_a(t)=\sum_nx[n]\,g(t-nT)$. That sum is the convolution of the impulse train $x_s(t)=\sum_nx[n]\delta(t-nT)$ of Lecture 17 with $g(t)$, so in frequency it is a product, $X_s(\Omega)G_a(\Omega)$. Since $X_s$ consists of copies of $X_a$ of height $\frac1T$ every $\frac{2\pi}{T}$, the right $G_a$ is an **ideal low-pass filter of height $T$ on $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$**: it keeps the central copy, deletes the others and undoes the $\frac1T$. Its inverse transform is the pulse $g(t)=\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)=\frac{\sin(\pi t/T)}{\pi t/T}$, which is $1$ at $t=0$ and $0$ at every other multiple of $T$, and the result is the **sinc interpolation formula** $x_a(t)=\sum_nx[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-nT)}{T}\right)$. It passes through every sample, and it returns the original bandlimited signal exactly when the copies did not overlap, i.e. when $f_s\gt2B$. In the frequency domain the ideal D/A takes one period of the DTFT, relabels $\omega$ as $\Omega=\omega/T$ and multiplies by $T$: $Y_a(\Omega)=T\,Y_d(\Omega T)$ for $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$. Put a digital filter between an ideal A/D and an ideal D/A and, as long as no aliasing reaches its passband, the whole chain acts on the analog input like the analog filter $H_d(\Omega T)$ on $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$ (and $0$ outside), with a digital cutoff $\omega_c$ becoming $\omega_c/T$ rad/s.

## 1. The reconstruction problem

[[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/17-ideal-analog-to-digital-conversion|Lecture 17]] went from $x_a(t)$ to $x[n]=x_a(nT)$ and found what that does to the spectrum: copies of $X_a$ every $\frac{2\pi}{T}$ rad/s, each scaled by $\frac1T$, with the Nyquist criterion $f_s\gt2B$ keeping them apart. Today goes the other way. Given the numbers $x[n]$ and the sampling period $T$ (in practice the same $T$ used to sample), recover $x_a(t)$ — for every $t$, not just at the sampling instants.

The first step is free (slide 4): **re-associate** each sample with its instant. Put $x[n]$ back at $t=nT$ as an impulse of area $x[n]$ and you have the impulse train of Lecture 17 again,

$$
x_s(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,\delta(t-nT),
$$

a continuous-time signal that knows the right values at the right instants and is zero everywhere else. The real question is the one the slide asks: what is $x_a(t)$ when $t\ne nT$? Filling in the values between known points is **interpolation**, "connecting the dots", and the notes write every interpolation scheme of this kind in one formula.

> [!key] The reconstruction formula (notes eq. 4, slide 5)
> $$
> x_a(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,g(t-nT).
> $$
> One copy of an **interpolating pulse** $g(t)$ per sample, centred at that sample's instant $nT$, scaled by its value $x[n]$, and all of them added. Choosing a D/A converter means choosing $g$.

The inked slide 5 tries the simplest pulse, a triangle of height $1$ that reaches zero at $\pm T$. Each copy $x[n]\,g(t-nT)$ is a tent standing on the sample $x[n]$, and between two neighbouring samples exactly two tents overlap, one falling and one rising, which add to the straight line joining the two samples:

Linear interpolation passes through every sample — any $g$ with $g(0)=1$ and $g(kT)=0$ for $k\ne0$ does — but so do infinitely many other curves. The samples alone cannot say which curve was sampled. What singles one out is an assumption from Lecture 17: $x_a$ was **bandlimited** and sampled fast enough. The rest of the lecture finds the $g$ that returns exactly that signal, and it is not the triangle.

## 2. Interpolation is a convolution

The reconstruction formula is a convolution in disguise. Convolving any $g(t)$ with a shifted impulse shifts it, $\delta(t-nT)*g(t)=g(t-nT)$, so

$$
\sum_nx[n]\,g(t-nT)=\sum_nx[n]\,\big(\delta(t-nT)*g(t)\big)=\Big(\sum_nx[n]\,\delta(t-nT)\Big)*g(t)=x_s(t)*g(t).
$$

The notes reach the same place through the integral (eqs. 5–7): because $x_s(\tau)$ is zero except at $\tau=nT$, the sum over $n$ is the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x_s(\tau)\,g(t-\tau)\,d\tau$, the **continuous-time convolution**. It is the discrete-time convolution of [[1-signals-and-systems/04-impulse-response-and-convolution|Lecture 4]] with an integral in place of the sum: flip $g$, shift it to $t$, multiply, add up. In other words, **a D/A converter is a continuous-time LTI filter with impulse response $g(t)$, driven by the impulse train $x_s(t)$**. Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, so:

> [!key] Interpolation in the frequency domain (notes eqs. 8–9)
> $$
> X_s(\Omega)\,G_a(\Omega)=\frac1T\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X_a\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}k\right)G_a(\Omega),\qquad g(t)\ \overset{\mathcal F}{\longleftrightarrow}\ G_a(\Omega).
> $$
> This is the spectrum of whatever the interpolator produces. We want it to be the spectrum of the signal that was sampled, $X_a(\Omega)$; the choice of $G_a$ decides whether it is.

> [!derivation]- The same statement for any sequence: $Y_d(\Omega T)\,G_a(\Omega)$
> Nothing above needs $x[n]$ to come from sampling. Feed any sequence $y[n]$ to the interpolator and transform term by term with $\delta(t-nT)\leftrightarrow e^{-j\Omega nT}$:
> $$
> y_a(t)=\sum_ny[n]\,g(t-nT)\ \overset{\mathcal F}{\longleftrightarrow}\ \sum_ny[n]\,e^{-j\Omega nT}\,G_a(\Omega)=Y_d(\Omega T)\,G_a(\Omega),
> $$
> because $\sum_ny[n]e^{-j(\Omega T)n}$ is the DTFT of $y[n]$ evaluated at $\omega=\Omega T$. This is the course review sheet's D/A entry, $Y_c(\Omega)=Y_d(\Omega T)P_c(\Omega)$, and it agrees with the box above since $X_s(\Omega)=X_d(\Omega T)$ ([[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/17-ideal-analog-to-digital-conversion|Lecture 17]] §5). It is the form §6 uses for the output of a digital filter.

## 3. The ideal interpolator: a low-pass filter of height T

The inked slide 6 puts the goal in one line, "what we want: $X_s(\Omega)\to\boxed{G_a(\Omega)}\to X_a(\Omega)$". Look at what $X_s$ holds: the copy $k=0$ is $\frac1TX_a(\Omega)$, and the copies $k\ne0$ sit at $\pm\frac{2\pi}{T},\pm\frac{4\pi}{T},\dots$ So $G_a$ has three jobs: **keep** the central copy, **delete** all the others, and **multiply by $T$** to undo the $\frac1T$. If $x_a$ is bandlimited to $B$ hertz and the copies do not overlap ($2\pi B\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$, the Nyquist criterion), the central copy lies inside $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$ and every other copy lies outside it, so a box does all three jobs:

> [!key] The ideal reconstruction filter (notes eq. 10, slide 8)
> $$
> G_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}T, & |\Omega|\le\dfrac{\pi}{T}\\[4pt] 0, & |\Omega|\gt\dfrac{\pi}{T}\end{cases}\;=\;T\,\operatorname{rect}\!\left(\frac{\Omega}{2\pi/T}\right).
> $$
> An **ideal low-pass filter** with cutoff $\frac{\pi}{T}$ rad/s (that is $\frac{f_s}{2}$ Hz, half the sampling rate) and passband gain $T$. Then $X_s(\Omega)G_a(\Omega)=T\cdot\frac1TX_a(\Omega)=X_a(\Omega)$.

The cutoff $\frac{\pi}{T}$ sits halfway between the centre of the central copy ($0$) and the centre of the next ($\frac{2\pi}{T}$). A signal sampled with a guard band would also survive any cutoff between $2\pi B$ and $\frac{2\pi}{T}-2\pi B$, but $\frac{\pi}{T}$ is the one choice that works for every signal satisfying the Nyquist criterion at this $T$, and it is the one the course calls *the* ideal D/A. The gain $T$ is the sampler's $\frac1T$ taken back: the two factors cancel in every A/D → D/A chain, which is why an output spectrum ends up with the heights of the input.

Now invert the box. A rectangle in frequency is a sinc in time:

> [!derivation]- The inverse CTFT of the box (notes eqs. 11–12)
> $$
> g(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/T}^{\pi/T}T\,e^{j\Omega t}\,d\Omega=\frac{T}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{e^{j\pi t/T}-e^{-j\pi t/T}}{jt}=\frac{T}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\sin(\pi t/T)}{t}=\frac{\sin(\pi t/T)}{\pi t/T}\qquad(t\ne0),
> $$
> and at $t=0$ the integral is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot T\cdot\frac{2\pi}{T}=1$, the limit of the same expression. (checked: numerical inverse CTFT at several $t$ for $T=2$, $0.1$ and $\frac{1}{80}$ s.)

> [!key] The ideal interpolating pulse (notes eqs. 12–13)
> $$
> g(t)=\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right),\qquad\text{with the course's}\quad\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\begin{cases}1, & u=0\\[2pt] \dfrac{\sin u}{u}, & u\ne0.\end{cases}
> $$
> $g(0)=1$, and $g(kT)=\frac{\sin(\pi k)}{\pi k}=0$ for every integer $k\ne0$.

The transform tables of the course and NumPy's `np.sinc` use the other sinc, $\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}$, in which the same pulse is written $\mathrm{sinc}(t/T)$. Same function, different bookkeeping: check which one a source means before you plug numbers in. The past Midterm 2 keys that write a sinc use $\frac{\sin x}{x}$, like the lecture: the [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] key writes $2\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(\frac{\pi}{T}(t-5T)\big)$. The past exams also write the analog signal $x_c(t)$, $X_c(\Omega)$, call the ideal converters "C/D" and "D/C" ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] #5), and write the Nyquist period $T_N$; all the same objects as here.

Three properties of $g$ matter later. Its zeros fall exactly on the other sampling instants, which is what makes the reconstruction pass through the samples (§4). Its main lobe is $2T$ wide and its side lobes decay only like $\frac1t$, so every sample influences the signal far away from its own instant. And it is **not causal**: $g(t)\ne0$ for $t\lt0$, and it never ends, so the ideal D/A cannot be built exactly; real converters approximate it (a hold circuit followed by an analog smoothing filter), which this course leaves out.

## 4. The sinc interpolation formula

Putting $g$ into the reconstruction formula gives the ideal D/A converter.

> [!key] Sinc interpolation (notes eq. 14, slide 7)
> $$
> x_a(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-nT)}{T}\right)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,\frac{\sin\big(\pi(t-nT)/T\big)}{\pi(t-nT)/T}.
> $$
> Each sample becomes a sinc centred at $t=nT$, as tall as the sample, with zeros at all the other sampling instants; the output is their sum.

What the formula guarantees, one property at a time:

- **It passes through every sample.** At $t=mT$ the pulse of sample $n$ is $\mathrm{sinc}\big(\pi(m-n)\big)$, which is $1$ for $n=m$ and $0$ otherwise, so $x_a(mT)=x[m]$. At a sampling instant only one pulse counts.
- **Between samples, all of them count.** Halfway between two samples, at $t=(m+\frac12)T$, the two neighbours get the weight $\mathrm{sinc}\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{2}{\pi}\approx0.64$ each, the next two $-\frac{2}{3\pi}\approx-0.21$, the next two $\frac{2}{5\pi}\approx0.13$, and so on with alternating signs. The interpolated value is a weighted sum of every sample, and it can overshoot both neighbours (practice 1 below).
- **The output is bandlimited to $\frac{\pi}{T}$ rad/s**, whatever sequence goes in: every pulse is, so the sum is. An ideal D/A at sampling rate $f_s$ can only produce frequencies below $\frac{f_s}{2}$.
- **It recovers $x_a(t)$ exactly** when $x[n]=x_a(nT)$ came from a signal bandlimited to $B$ hertz sampled at $f_s\gt2B$: the copies did not overlap, $G_a$ cuts out the central one, and $x_a(t)=\sum_nx_a(nT)\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-nT)}{T}\right)$ for every $t$. The samples of a bandlimited signal hold all of it, and this is the formula that gets it back.

The notes close with the assumption all of this rests on: the central copy must be separable from the others. Below the Nyquist rate the copies overlap, the box cuts out a sum of copies instead of one, and the D/A faithfully rebuilds the wrong signal; that is **aliasing**, the subject of [[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/19-aliasing|Lecture 19]]. Exactly at $f_s=2B$ the copies touch at $\pm\frac{\pi}{T}$, and the recovery is exact only if $X_a$ has nothing sitting right at the band edge. A triangle that has fallen to zero at $2\pi B$ comes back perfectly ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #5(b) at $T=T_N$); a sinusoid at exactly $B$ hertz does not — $\sin(\Omega_0t)$ sampled at $f_s=\Omega_0/\pi$ gives $x[n]=\sin(\pi n)=0$, and the D/A returns $0$. Lecture 19 makes this precise.

> [!intuition] Why not just connect the dots?
> The triangle of §1 is a legitimate $g$, and its transform is $T\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega T}{2}\right)$. Compare it with the box: at the band edge $\frac{\pi}{T}$ it has dropped to $\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^2T\approx0.41T$ instead of $T$, so it **droops** across the central copy; and it vanishes only at the image centres $\frac{2\pi k}{T}$ — at $\frac{3\pi}{2T}$, inside the band of the first image, it is still $\frac{8}{9\pi^2}T\approx0.09T$ instead of $0$, so it **lets a little of every copy through** (checked: numerical CTFT of the triangle). Linear interpolation distorts what it should keep and keeps some of what it should delete. The sinc is the one pulse whose spectrum is flat on the central copy and zero on all the others — the reason the Python in §11 rebuilds a signal $40$ times more accurately with sincs than with straight lines from the same samples.

> $$
> x_a(1)=\frac{6}{\pi}-\frac{2}{7\pi}=\frac{40}{7\pi}\approx1.82,\quad x_a(3)=\frac{32}{5\pi}\approx2.04,\quad x_a(5)=-\frac{8}{3\pi}\approx-0.85,\quad x_a(7)=\frac{16}{5\pi}\approx1.02,\quad x_a(9)=\frac{8}{7\pi}\approx0.36,

>

## 6. The D/A in the frequency domain

§2's derivation gives the output spectrum of the ideal D/A for **any** sequence $y[n]$, sampled or not: $Y_a(\Omega)=Y_d(\Omega T)\,G_a(\Omega)$, and $G_a$ is $T$ inside $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$ and $0$ outside.

> [!key] What the ideal D/A does to a spectrum
> $$
> Y_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}T\,Y_d(\Omega T), & |\Omega|\lt\dfrac{\pi}{T}\\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
> Take **one period** of the DTFT, $-\pi\lt\omega\lt\pi$; **relabel** the axis by $\Omega=\omega/T$ (so $\omega=\pi$ lands at $\frac{\pi}{T}$); **multiply** the height by $T$; and set everything outside $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$ to zero. A digital frequency $\omega$ comes out as the analog frequency $\Omega=\dfrac{\omega}{T}$ rad/s, i.e. $f=\dfrac{\omega}{2\pi T}=\dfrac{\omega}{2\pi}f_s$ Hz.

This is Lecture 17's $\omega=\Omega T$ run backwards, with one difference: sampling sends every $\Omega$ somewhere, but the D/A only uses $|\omega|\lt\pi$, so it can never produce more than half the sampling rate. A tone shows it most simply: $y[n]=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)$ through an ideal D/A with $T=1$ ms comes out as $\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi/4}{10^{-3}}t\right)=\cos(250\pi t)$, a $125$ Hz cosine. And if $y[n]=x_a(nT)$ came straight from an A/D with no overlap, $Y_d(\Omega T)=\frac1TX_a(\Omega)$ on $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$ and $Y_a=X_a$: an ideal A/D followed by an ideal D/A, above the Nyquist rate, is the identity.

> [!question] Reading the samples off a D/A output
> An ideal D/A with $f_s=1$ kHz produces $y_a(t)=\cos(600\pi t)+3$. What sequence went in?

> [!success]- Answer (checked)
> The ideal D/A passes through its samples, so $y[n]=y_a(nT)=\cos(600\pi\cdot10^{-3}n)+3=\cos(0.6\pi n)+3$. In frequency: the impulses at $\Omega=\pm600\pi$ came from $\omega=\pm600\pi T=\pm0.6\pi$, inside $(-\pi,\pi)$, and the constant from $\omega=0$. The input is unique, because a D/A output determines its samples. [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2]] #9(a) is this question with $2\cos(300\pi t)$ at $400$ Hz: $x[n]=2\cos\!\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right)$. Its part (b), which inputs *could have produced* that output, is about aliasing and belongs to Lecture 19.

> [!recipe] Three sketches
> 1. **$X_d(\omega)$**, as in Lecture 17: scale the axis by $T$ (an edge $\Omega_{\max}$ lands at $\Omega_{\max}T$), copy every $2\pi$, scale the heights by $\frac1T$, add where copies overlap.
> 2. **$Y_d(\omega)=H_d(\omega)X_d(\omega)$**, period by period ([[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]]). $H_d$ is $2\pi$-periodic, so $Y_d$ still has a copy around every multiple of $2\pi$.
> 3. **$Y_a(\Omega)$** from one period of $Y_d$: relabel $\Omega=\omega/T$, multiply by $T$, zero outside $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$. Label the cutoff in rad/s, $\omega_c/T$.

> [!question] Slide 10 — $T=\frac{1}{80}$ s
> $X_a(\Omega)$ is a semicircle of height $1$ out to $\pm60\pi$ rad/s, $X_a(\Omega)=\sqrt{1-(\Omega/60\pi)^2}$ for $|\Omega|\le60\pi$. $H_d(\omega)$ is an ideal low-pass with cutoff $\omega_c=\frac{\pi}{2}$ (gain $1$). With $T=\frac{1}{80}$ s, sketch (a) $X_d(\omega)$, (b) $Y_d(\omega)$, (c) $Y_a(\Omega)$.

> [!success]- Answer — matches the inked slide 10 (checked)
> **(a)** The edge lands at $\omega_{\max}=\Omega_{\max}T=60\pi\cdot\frac{1}{80}=\frac{3\pi}{4}$, the height is $\frac1T=80$. $X_d$ is a semicircle of height $80$ on $|\omega|\le\frac{3\pi}{4}$, repeated at every $2\pi k$ (the ink colours the $k=\pm1$ copies, centred at $\pm2\pi$). The next copy starts at $2\pi-\frac{3\pi}{4}=\frac{5\pi}{4}$: a guard band, no overlap ($80$ Hz is above the Nyquist rate of $60$ Hz).
>
> **(b)** $Y_d=H_dX_d$ keeps $|\omega|\le\frac{\pi}{2}$ of every copy: the top of the semicircle, height $80$ at $\omega=0$ and $80\sqrt{1-(2/3)^2}=\frac{80\sqrt5}{3}\approx59.6$ at the cut $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$, then zero out to the next copy's cut piece around $2\pi$.
>
> **(c)** Keep $|\omega|\lt\pi$, relabel $\Omega=\omega/T=80\,\omega$, multiply by $T=\frac{1}{80}$: the cutoff $\frac{\pi}{2}$ becomes $\Omega_c=\frac{\pi/2}{1/80}=40\pi$ rad/s ($20$ Hz), and the height is back to $1$:
> $$
> Y_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1-(\Omega/60\pi)^2}, & |\Omega|\le40\pi\\ 0, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
> $$
> the original semicircle cut at $\pm40\pi$, with value $\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\approx0.75$ at the cut.

> [!question] Slide 11 — the same system with $T=\frac{1}{50}$ s
> Same $X_a$ and $H_d$. Sketch (a) $X_d(\omega)$, (b) $Y_d(\omega)$, (c) $Y_a(\Omega)$.

> [!success]- Answer — matches the inked slide 11 (checked)
> **(a)** Now $\omega_{\max}=60\pi\cdot\frac{1}{50}=\frac{6\pi}{5}\gt\pi$ and the height is $50$. The copy centred at $2\pi$ starts at $2\pi-\frac{6\pi}{5}=\frac{4\pi}{5}$, so neighbouring copies **overlap on $\frac{4\pi}{5}\le|\omega|\le\frac{6\pi}{5}$** ("Aliasing!" in the ink): $50$ Hz is below the Nyquist rate of $60$ Hz. There $X_d$ is the sum of two arcs: it has a sharp corner at $\frac{4\pi}{5}$, value $\frac{50\sqrt5}{3}\approx37.3$, where the next arc enters vertically, and rises to a rounded maximum of $2\cdot50\sqrt{1-\frac{25}{36}}=\frac{50\sqrt{11}}{3}\approx55.3$ at $\pm\pi$ (the two arcs' slopes cancel there), higher than the central peak.
>
> **(b)** The filter keeps $|\omega|\le\frac{\pi}{2}$, and $\frac{\pi}{2}\lt\frac{4\pi}{5}$: the overlap lies entirely in the stopband. $Y_d$ is the clean top of the central semicircle, height $50$, cut at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ where it is $50\sqrt{1-(5/12)^2}\approx45.5$ (the cut lands at $\Omega=\frac{\pi/2}{T}=25\pi$, and $\frac{25\pi}{60\pi}=\frac{5}{12}$), repeated every $2\pi$.
>
> **(c)** Relabel $\Omega=50\,\omega$ and multiply by $\frac{1}{50}$: $Y_a$ is the original semicircle cut at $\pm25\pi$ rad/s ($12.5$ Hz), height $1$, value $\frac{\sqrt{119}}{12}\approx0.91$ at the cut. **Undistorted**, although the A/D aliased: the filter threw the aliased band away before the D/A could rebuild it.

The two slides make the lecture's last point. The digital filter is the same in both, but the analog filter the chain implements is not: a cutoff of $\frac{\pi}{2}$ is $40\pi$ rad/s at $80$ Hz and $25\pi$ rad/s at $50$ Hz.

> [!key] The effective analog filter
> If no aliasing reaches the passband of $H_d$, then for $|\Omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{T}$
> $$
> Y_a(\Omega)=T\,H_d(\Omega T)\,X_d(\Omega T)=T\,H_d(\Omega T)\cdot\frac1TX_a(\Omega)=H_d(\Omega T)\,X_a(\Omega),
> $$
> and $Y_a=0$ outside. The chain acts on the analog input like the analog LTI filter
> $$
> H_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}H_d(\Omega T), & |\Omega|\lt\dfrac{\pi}{T}\\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
> A digital cutoff $\omega_c$ becomes $\Omega_c=\dfrac{\omega_c}{T}$ rad/s, i.e. $f_c=\dfrac{\omega_c}{2\pi}f_s$ Hz: change $f_s$ and the same digital filter becomes a different analog one. To *design* $H_d$ for a wanted $H_a$, read it backwards: $H_d(\omega)=H_a(\omega/T)$ on $|\omega|\le\pi$.

When is "no aliasing reaches the passband" true? Always, if $f_s\gt2B$: nothing overlaps at all. But slide 11 shows the weaker condition that is enough for a low-pass: the first foreign copy starts at $2\pi-\Omega_{\max}T$, and it only has to start beyond the cutoff,

$$
\omega_c\le2\pi-\Omega_{\max}T .
$$

For slide 11 that is $\frac{\pi}{2}\le2\pi-60\pi T$, i.e. $T\le\frac{1}{40}$ s: the same filter keeps the output clean down to $40$ Hz, two thirds of the Nyquist rate (checked: undistorted at $T=\frac{1}{41}$ and $\frac{1}{40}$ s, distorted at $\frac{1}{38}$ s). "The largest $T$ for which the system still does its job" is a favourite exam question, built on exactly this inequality; what the aliased band looks like when it does reach the output is Lecture 19.

## 8. HW7 #4: the same chain with a triangle

HW7 #4 is slides 10–11 with a triangle instead of a semicircle: $X_a(\Omega)=1-\frac{|\Omega|}{50\pi}$ on $|\Omega|\le50\pi$ (Nyquist rate $50$ Hz), the same ideal low-pass $\omega_c=\frac{\pi}{2}$, at $T=\frac{1}{50}$ s and $T=\frac{1}{40}$ s. ## 9. Practice

> [!question] Practice 1 — values between the samples
> $x[n]=2\delta[n]-\delta[n-2]$ goes through an ideal D/A with $T=0.1$ s. Find (a) $x_a(0)$, $x_a(0.1)$, $x_a(0.2)$; (b) $x_a(0.05)$ and $x_a(0.15)$; (c) $x_a(0.25)$. Is $x_a(0.15)$ between the two samples it sits between?

> [!success]- Answer (checked: the sinc sum evaluated directly)
> $x_a(t)=2\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{0.1}\right)-\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-0.2)}{0.1}\right)$.
>
> **(a)** At the sampling instants the D/A returns the samples: $x_a(0)=2$, $x_a(0.1)=x[1]=0$, $x_a(0.2)=-1$.
>
> **(b)** $t=0.05$: the arguments are $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $-\frac{3\pi}{2}$, with $\mathrm{sinc}\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{2}{\pi}$ and $\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(-\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)=\frac{\sin(-3\pi/2)}{-3\pi/2}=\frac{1}{-3\pi/2}=-\frac{2}{3\pi}$, so $x_a(0.05)=\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{2}{3\pi}=\frac{14}{3\pi}\approx1.49$. $t=0.15$: arguments $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and $-\frac{\pi}{2}$, so $x_a(0.15)=2\left(-\frac{2}{3\pi}\right)-\frac{2}{\pi}=-\frac{10}{3\pi}\approx-1.06$.
>
> **(c)** Arguments $\frac{5\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$: $x_a(0.25)=2\cdot\frac{2}{5\pi}-\frac{2}{\pi}=-\frac{6}{5\pi}\approx-0.38$.
>
> No: $x_a(0.15)\approx-1.06$ lies below both $x_a(0.1)=0$ and $x_a(0.2)=-1$. Sinc interpolation is not connecting the dots; the far sample $x[0]=2$ pulls the curve through its negative side lobe.

> [!question] Practice 2 — a digital high-pass between the converters
> $X_a(\Omega)=1-\frac{|\Omega|}{300\pi}$ on $|\Omega|\le300\pi$ (a triangle, $B=150$ Hz) goes through ideal A/D → $H_d$ → ideal D/A, where $H_d(\omega)=1$ for $\frac{\pi}{2}\le|\omega|\le\pi$ and $0$ for $|\omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{2}$. (a) Sketch $X_d$, $Y_d$, $Y_a$ for $T=\frac{1}{400}$ s. Is the chain an analog high-pass? (b) Repeat for $T=\frac{1}{200}$ s. (c) What is the largest $T$ for which $Y_a(\Omega)=H_d(\Omega T)X_a(\Omega)$?

> [!success]- Answer (checked: periodized spectra and the D/A rule on a grid)
> **(a)** Edge $300\pi\cdot\frac{1}{400}=\frac{3\pi}{4}$, height $400$, guard band $\frac{3\pi}{4}\lt|\omega|\lt\frac{5\pi}{4}$. $Y_d$ keeps the two outer slopes, $\frac{\pi}{2}\le|\omega|\le\frac{3\pi}{4}$, from $\frac{400}{3}\approx133.3$ down to $0$. The D/A maps $\frac{\pi}{2}\mapsto200\pi$ and $\pi\mapsto400\pi$: $Y_a(\Omega)=X_a(\Omega)$ for $200\pi\le|\Omega|\le300\pi$ (from $\frac13$ down to $0$) and $0$ below $200\pi$. So it acts as $H_a(\Omega)=1$ on $200\pi\le|\Omega|\lt400\pi$, $0$ elsewhere: a **band-pass** from $100$ to $200$ Hz, not a high-pass, because the D/A cannot produce anything above $\frac{f_s}{2}=200$ Hz. For this input the difference is invisible, since $X_a$ ends at $150$ Hz.
>
> **(b)** Edge $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, height $200$; copies overlap on $\frac{\pi}{2}\le|\omega|\le\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and the two slopes add to the constant $200\left(2-\frac{2\pi}{3\pi/2}\right)=\frac{400}{3}$. That band is exactly what the high-pass keeps: $Y_d=\frac{400}{3}$ on $\frac{\pi}{2}\le|\omega|\le\pi$. The D/A ($\Omega=200\,\omega$, height $\times\frac{1}{200}$) gives $Y_a=\frac23$ on $100\pi\le|\Omega|\lt200\pi$ and $0$ elsewhere — flat, while $X_a$ falls from $\frac23$ to $\frac13$ over the same band. **Distorted**: here the aliasing lands in the passband.
>
> **(c)** A high-pass keeps the band next to $\pi$, where overlap begins, so no overlap is allowed at all: $2\pi-300\pi T\ge\pi$, i.e. $T\le\frac{1}{300}$ s, the Nyquist period. The low-pass trick of §7 does not help a high-pass ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] #5(b) makes the same point).

> [!question] Practice 3 — the analog cutoff
> A digital low-pass with $\omega_c=0.3\pi$ sits between an ideal A/D and an ideal D/A, and the input is bandlimited to $3$ kHz. (a) At $f_s=8$ kHz, what analog cutoff does the chain have, in rad/s and in Hz? (b) What $f_s$ (and $T$) puts the cutoff at $1$ kHz with the same filter? (c) At $8$ kHz, would the answer to (a) still hold for an input bandlimited to $5$ kHz?

> [!success]- Answer (checked)
> **(a)** $\Omega_c=\frac{\omega_c}{T}=0.3\pi\cdot8000=2400\pi$ rad/s, $f_c=\frac{\omega_c}{2\pi}f_s=0.15\cdot8000=1200$ Hz. ($8$ kHz is above the $6$ kHz Nyquist rate, so nothing aliases.)
>
> **(b)** $f_s=\frac{2\pi f_c}{\omega_c}=\frac{2\pi\cdot1000}{0.3\pi}\approx6667$ Hz, $T=0.15$ ms. Still above $2B=6$ kHz, so the chain is the analog low-pass $H_a(\Omega)=1$ for $|\Omega|\le2000\pi$.
>
> **(c)** Yes. Now $8$ kHz is below the $10$ kHz Nyquist rate, but the first foreign copy starts at $2\pi-2\pi\cdot\frac{5000}{8000}=0.75\pi\gt0.3\pi$: the aliasing stays in the stopband and the output is the input low-passed at $1200$ Hz.

## 10. Traps

> [!trap] Where points go on D/A questions
> - **Two sincs.** The course's $\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin u}{u}$ makes the pulse $\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)$; with the tables' and NumPy's $\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}$ it is $\mathrm{sinc}(t/T)$. Mixing them moves every zero crossing by a factor of $\pi$.
> - **One sample is one sinc, never an impulse.** "$x[n]=2\delta[n-5]$ gives $x_a(t)=2\delta(t-5T)$" is False ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #1(c), [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] #1(e)): the ideal D/A outputs $2\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-5T)}{T}\right)$.
> - **Put the peak at $t=nT$.** $\delta[n-3]$ peaks at $t=3T$; check any sketch with $x_a(nT)=x[n]$ (the posted [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2019|FA2019 MT2]] #8(b) key draws it at $4T$, a known slip).
> - **The height $T$.** The D/A multiplies by $T$ and so undoes the $\frac1T$ of the sampler; $Y_a$ has the heights of $X_a$, not of $X_d$.
> - **$Y_a$ is one copy and stops at $\pm\frac{\pi}{T}$.** $Y_d$ repeats every $2\pi$; $Y_a$ does not repeat, and nothing in it lies beyond $\frac{\pi}{T}$ — at twice the Nyquist period the output of [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #5(b) stops at $500\pi$, half the input's band.
> - **The analog cutoff depends on $T$:** $\Omega_c=\omega_c/T$. Writing the digital $\frac{\pi}{2}$ on the $\Omega$ axis is the commonest slip.
> - **A digital high-pass is an analog band-pass** between converters, with upper edge $\frac{\pi}{T}$ (practice 2).
> - **"Aliasing happened" is not "the output is wrong".** If the overlap lands in the stopband the output is clean (slide 11, HW7 #4(c)); if it lands in the passband it is not (practice 2(b)).
> - **The ideal reconstruction filter is not causal** ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #1(c), False): $g(t)$ is nonzero for $t\lt0$.
> - **Exactly at the Nyquist rate** recovery depends on the band edge: a spectrum that is zero there comes back, a sinusoid at exactly $B$ hertz may not ([[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/19-aliasing|Lecture 19]]).

## 11. In Python

Sinc interpolation needs infinitely many samples; a computer has finitely many. Truncating the sum to $|n|\le N$ leaves an error that shrinks slowly (the sinc tails decay like $\frac1t$), but it shrinks. The test signal has tones at $3$ and $7$ Hz ($B=7$ Hz) and is sampled at $20$ Hz; NumPy's `np.sinc(u)` is $\frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u}$, so the course's $\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)$ is `np.sinc(t / T)`.

```python
import numpy as np

# a bandlimited test signal: tones at 3 Hz and 7 Hz, so B = 7 Hz and the Nyquist rate is 14 Hz
xa = lambda t: np.cos(2 * np.pi * 3 * t) + 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 7 * t + 1)

fs = 20.0                                # sample above the Nyquist rate
T = 1 / fs
t = np.linspace(-1, 1, 2001)             # where we rebuild x_a(t): between the samples too

def sinc_da(x, n, t, T):
    """ideal D/A: sum_n x[n] sinc(pi (t - nT)/T); np.sinc(u) = sin(pi u)/(pi u), so sinc(pi t/T) is np.sinc(t/T)"""
    return np.sum(x[:, None] * np.sinc((t[None, :] - n[:, None] * T) / T), axis=0)

for N in (50, 500, 5000):                # keep only the samples n = -N, ..., N
    n = np.arange(-N, N + 1)
    x = xa(n * T)                        # x[n] = x_a(nT)
    err = np.max(np.abs(sinc_da(x, n, t, T) - xa(t)))
    print(f"sinc interpolation with |n| <= {N:4d}: max error on [-1, 1] = {err:.1e}")

n = np.arange(-50, 51)
lin = np.interp(t, n * T, xa(n * T))     # "connect the dots" with straight lines instead
print(f"linear interpolation (same samples):    max error on [-1, 1] = {np.max(np.abs(lin - xa(t))):.1e}")
```

```text
sinc interpolation with |n| <=   50: max error on [-1, 1] = 7.9e-03
sinc interpolation with |n| <=  500: max error on [-1, 1] = 3.7e-04
sinc interpolation with |n| <= 5000: max error on [-1, 1] = 3.4e-05
linear interpolation (same samples):    max error on [-1, 1] = 3.4e-01
```

From $101$ samples the sincs already rebuild the signal between the samples to within $0.008$, and the error keeps falling as more samples are used; straight lines through the same $101$ samples miss by $0.34$, about forty times more, and adding samples outside $[-1,1]$ would not help them at all. Rerun it with `fs = 12.0`, below the $14$ Hz Nyquist rate, and the sinc error no longer goes to zero however large $N$ is: the $7$ Hz tone comes back at $12-7=5$ Hz (checked in `verify/L18_lecture.py`). That is Lecture 19.
