> [!abstract] In one breath
> Sampling a continuous-time signal every $T$ seconds, $x[n]=x_a(nT)$, is modelled by multiplying $x_a(t)$ by an impulse train. In the frequency domain that multiplication becomes a sum of **shifted copies** of the spectrum: $X_s(\Omega)=\frac1T\sum_n X_a\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}n\right)$, one copy every $2\pi/T$ rad/s, each scaled by $1/T$. Renaming the frequency axis by $\omega=\Omega T$ turns this into the DTFT of the sequence, $X_d(\omega)=\frac1T\sum_n X_a\!\left(\frac{\omega-2\pi n}{T}\right)$, copies every $2\pi$ as every DTFT must have. If $x_a$ is **bandlimited** to $B$ hertz ($X_a(\Omega)=0$ for $|\Omega|\gt2\pi B$), the copies stay apart exactly when the right edge of one is left of the left edge of the next: $2\pi B\lt\frac{2\pi}{T}-2\pi B$, i.e. $f_s=\frac1T\gt2B$. That is the **Nyquist criterion**; $2B$ is the **Nyquist rate**. Sample faster and a guard band separates the copies; sample at exactly $2B$ and they touch; sample slower and they overlap and add into a new shape, which is **aliasing** (the next lectures). Sampling a sinusoid shows the seed of it: $\cos(\Omega_0t)$ becomes $\cos(\omega_0n)$ with $\omega_0=\Omega_0T$, and every $\Omega_0$ whose $\Omega_0T$ differs by a multiple of $2\pi$ gives the very same samples.

## 1. Ideal sampling

[[1-signals-and-systems/01-digital-signals|Lecture 1]] defined a discrete-time signal as the samples of a continuous-time one: with **sampling period** $T$ (seconds per sample) and **sampling frequency** $f_s=1/T$ (samples per second, hertz),

$$
x[n]=x_a(nT),\qquad-\infty\lt n\lt\infty .
$$

Ideal sampling measures $x_a(t)$ instantaneously at $t=0,\pm T,\pm2T,\dots$ and keeps nothing else. (The notes write the analog signal $x(t)$ and its spectrum $X_a(\Omega)$; the slides and this site write $x_a(t)$, $X_a(\Omega)$; the past exams write either $x_a(t)$, $X_a(\Omega)$ or $x_c(t)$, $X_c(\Omega)$, sometimes both on one exam — all the same thing.) Since Lecture 1 we have treated $x[n]$ as given. Today's question, from slide 3, is what the choice of $T$ does:

> [!question] Key question of the lecture
> How does the sampling period $T$ affect the representation $x[n]$ of $x_a(t)$? Are there safe choices of $T$ that guarantee $x_a(t)$ can be recovered from $x[n]$?

## 2. The impulse-train model

Taking one value of a function is what a Dirac impulse does. The sifting property (the same one that let [[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]] write $\delta(\omega-\omega_0)H_d(\omega)=H_d(\omega_0)\,\delta(\omega-\omega_0)$),

$$
x_a(t)\,\delta(t-t_0)=x_a(t_0)\,\delta(t-t_0),
$$

says that multiplying by an impulse at $t_0$ keeps only the value $x_a(t_0)$, as the area of the impulse. Multiply instead by a whole **impulse train**, one impulse every $T$ seconds:

> [!key] The ideally sampled signal
> $$
> x_s(t)=x_a(t)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x_a(nT)\,\delta(t-nT)
> $$
> (the notes call it $x_{\text{sampled}}(t)$). It is **still a continuous-time signal**, defined for every $t$: zero between the sampling instants, with an impulse of *area* $x_a(nT)$ at each $t=nT$. In any interval of width $T$ it carries exactly one number — between $\tfrac{3T}{2}$ and $\tfrac{5T}{2}$ only the impulse at $t=2T$, of area $x_a(2T)$.

Why the detour through $x_s(t)$ instead of going straight to $x[n]$? Because $x_s(t)$ has a CTFT, so we can ask what sampling does to a spectrum using the continuous-time properties we already have; and because $x[n]$ holds the same numbers as $x_s(t)$, the areas $x_a(nT)$ re-indexed by $n$, so whatever we learn about $X_s(\Omega)$ transfers to $X_d(\omega)$ with one change of variable (§5). The notes' Figure 1, redrawn above, is the whole ideal A/D converter: multiply, then store.

## 3. The spectrum of the sampled signal

The impulse train is periodic with period $T$, so its spectrum is a train of impulses too, spaced by $2\pi/T$ rad/s and each of area $2\pi/T$ (the pair is written out on slide 5; it is the Fourier series of the train, [[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]], with every coefficient equal to $1/T$). Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, and convolving with an impulse shifts. The result:

> [!key] The sampled spectrum (notes eq. 11)
> $$
> x_s(t)\ \overset{\mathcal F}{\longleftrightarrow}\ X_s(\Omega)=\frac1T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}X_a\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}\,n\right).
> $$
> Sampling at period $T$ **copies** the analog spectrum every $\dfrac{2\pi}{T}$ rad/s (every $f_s$ hertz) and **scales** every copy by $\dfrac1T$. Where copies overlap they add.

> [!derivation]- Through the windowing property (notes eqs. 6–11, slide 5)
> The windowing (multiplication-in-time) property of the CTFT is the continuous-time version of the one in Lecture 14: $x_a(t)\,f(t)\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2\pi}X_a(\Omega)*F_a(\Omega)$. Take $f(t)=\sum_n\delta(t-nT)$, whose transform is $F_a(\Omega)=\frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_n\delta\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}n\right)$. Then
> $$
> X_s(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[X_a(\Omega)*\frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_n\delta\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}n\right)\right]=\frac1T\sum_n X_a(\Omega)*\delta\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}n\right)=\frac1T\sum_nX_a\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi}{T}n\right),
> $$
> because convolving with $\delta(\Omega-\Omega_0)$ returns the function shifted to $\Omega_0$. The $2\pi$ of the windowing property cancels the $2\pi$ in the train's transform and leaves the $\frac1T$.

> [!intuition] Why copies, and why $1/T$
> Write the impulse train as its Fourier series, $\sum_n\delta(t-nT)=\frac1T\sum_k e^{j\frac{2\pi}{T}kt}$: a periodic signal is a sum of harmonics, here all of equal weight $\frac1T$ (the train's period is $T$, and each coefficient is $\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\delta(t)e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{T}kt}dt=\frac1T$). Multiplying $x_a(t)$ by $e^{j\Omega_kt}$ shifts its spectrum to $\Omega_k$ (the frequency-shift property), so multiplying by the train produces one shifted copy per harmonic, at $\Omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}k$, each weighted by $\frac1T$. A faster sampler (smaller $T$) pushes the copies farther apart *and* makes them taller: impulses that come closer together in time have harmonics of larger weight $\frac1T$.

Two things to hold on to before the examples. First, the copies are an exact description, not an approximation: $X_s(\Omega)$ really is periodic in $\Omega$ with period $2\pi/T$, even though $X_a$ was not. Second, if the copies do not overlap, the central one ($n=0$) is $\frac1TX_a(\Omega)$ exactly, so nothing about $x_a$ has been lost; if they do overlap, the sum no longer lets you read off $X_a$. Everything in §6 is about keeping them apart.

## 4. Example 1: varying the sampling period

> [!question] Slide 6 — the same spectrum, two sampling periods
> Let $X_a(\Omega)=\operatorname{rect}\!\left(\dfrac{\Omega}{20\pi}\right)$, the box of height 1 on $|\Omega|\le10\pi$ (a bandlimit of $5$ Hz). Sketch $X_s(\Omega)$ (a) for $T=\frac{1}{20}$ s on $-100\pi\lt\Omega\lt100\pi$, and (b) for $T=\frac{1}{10}$ s on $-50\pi\lt\Omega\lt50\pi$.

> [!success]- Answer (a) — matches Prof. Snyder's in-class sketch (checked: copies summed on a grid)
> The spacing is $\frac{2\pi}{T}=40\pi$ and the height $\frac1T=20$. On the requested window the copies $n=-2,\dots,2$ are boxes of height $20$ and width $20\pi$ centred at $-80\pi,-40\pi,0,40\pi,80\pi$: the $n=0$ copy occupies $|\Omega|\le10\pi$, the $n=1$ copy $30\pi\le\Omega\le50\pi$, and so on. Between neighbouring boxes there is a **gap of $20\pi$** ($10\pi$ to $30\pi$, for instance) where $X_s=0$. Each copy is a scaled replica of $X_a$: no overlap, nothing lost.

> [!success]- Answer (b) — matches the in-class sketch (checked)
> Now $\frac{2\pi}{T}=20\pi$ and $\frac1T=10$: boxes of height $10$ and width $20\pi$ centred at $0,\pm20\pi,\pm40\pi$. > $$
> X_s(\Omega)=10\quad\text{for all }\Omega .
> $$
> The boxes have merged into something that no longer looks like $X_a$ — and yet no two copies share any interval, so this is the borderline "at the Nyquist rate" case of §6: $f_s=10$ Hz $=2\cdot5$ Hz. ## 5. From analog to digital frequency: the DTFT of the samples

$X_s(\Omega)$ is the spectrum of a continuous-time signal, in radians per second. What we store is the sequence $x[n]$, whose DTFT $X_d(\omega)$ lives in radians per sample on $[-\pi,\pi]$. The two axes are related through the one quantity both signals share, the sampling period. Slide 7 finds the relation by sampling a sinusoid:

$$
x_a(t)=\cos(\Omega_0t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x[n]=x_a(nT)=\cos(\Omega_0nT)=\cos(\Omega_0T\,n)=\cos(\omega_0n),\qquad\omega_0=\Omega_0T .
$$

> [!key] Analog and digital frequency
> $$
> \omega=\Omega\,T\qquad\left(\frac{\text{rad}}{\text{sample}}=\frac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}}\cdot\frac{\text{s}}{\text{sample}}\right),\qquad\text{equivalently}\quad\omega=\frac{\Omega}{f_s},\quad\Omega=\frac{\omega}{T}.
> $$
> An analog frequency of $\Omega$ rad/s, or $f=\Omega/2\pi$ Hz, lands at the digital frequency $\omega=\Omega T=2\pi f/f_s$. Half the sampling rate, $f=f_s/2$, lands at $\omega=\pi$, the edge of the DTFT's period.

Substituting $\Omega=\omega/T$ into the copies formula gives the DTFT after ideal A/D conversion:

> [!key] The DTFT of the samples (notes eqs. 15–16)
> $$
> X_d(\omega)=X_s(\Omega)\Big|_{\Omega=\omega/T}=\frac1T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}X_a\!\left(\frac{\omega-2\pi n}{T}\right).
> $$
> The copies now sit every $2\pi$ — a DTFT is $2\pi$-periodic, and this is where that periodicity comes from for a sampled signal — and each is the analog spectrum with its axis scaled by $T$ and its height by $\frac1T$. When the copies do not overlap, on one period $X_d(\omega)=\frac1T\,X_a\!\left(\frac{\omega}{T}\right)$ for $|\omega|\le\pi$ (the course review sheet's form; its $\frac{\omega+2k\pi}{T}$ is the same sum with $k=-n$).

> [!derivation]- Why substituting $\Omega=\omega/T$ is legitimate
> The cosine argument shows what the substitution must be; this shows it is exact for any signal. Transform $x_s(t)=\sum_nx_a(nT)\delta(t-nT)$ term by term with the CTFT of a shifted impulse, $\delta(t-nT)\leftrightarrow e^{-j\Omega nT}$:
> $$
> X_s(\Omega)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x_a(nT)\,e^{-j\Omega nT}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,e^{-j(\Omega T)\,n}=X_d(\omega)\Big|_{\omega=\Omega T}.
> $$
> The middle expression is literally the DTFT sum of $x[n]$ evaluated at $\omega=\Omega T$. So $X_d(\omega)=X_s(\omega/T)$, and the copies formula for $X_s$ becomes the one for $X_d$. (Singer & Munson §3.2 derive the same relation the other way round, by writing $x[n]=x_a(nT)$ as the inverse CTFT integral and cutting it into pieces of length $2\pi$ in $\omega$.)

> [!recipe] From a sketch of $X_a(\Omega)$ to a sketch of $X_d(\omega)$
> 1. **Scale the axis by $T$.** Every analog frequency $\Omega$ moves to $\omega=\Omega T$; a band edge $2\pi B$ moves to $2\pi BT$. Mark where $\omega=\pm\pi$ (that is $\Omega=\pm\pi/T$, or $\pm f_s/2$ in hertz) falls relative to the band edge.
> 2. **Copy every $2\pi$.** Draw the scaled spectrum centred at $0,\pm2\pi,\pm4\pi,\dots$
> 3. **Scale the heights by $1/T$** — a height of $1$ becomes $f_s$. Label it.
> 4. **Add where copies overlap.** Do not draw them side by side; the DTFT is the sum, and overlapping pieces of straight lines add to new straight lines (Example 2(c)).
> 5. **Read off one period**, $-\pi\le\omega\le\pi$, which is what an exam asks for; the rest repeats.

## 6. Bandlimited signals and the Nyquist criterion

The copies in $X_s(\Omega)$ are $2\pi/T$ apart. If $T$ is too large they crowd together and overlap. For a general signal nothing can be said; for a **bandlimited** one, the overlap can be prevented.

> [!key] Bandlimited signals
> $x_a(t)$ is bandlimited to $B$ hertz (bandlimit $2\pi B$ rad/s) if
> $$
> X_a(\Omega)=0\qquad\text{for }|\Omega|\gt2\pi B .
> $$
> $B$ is the highest linear frequency present (the notes' eq. 17; the slide writes $|X_a(\Omega)|=0$ for $|\Omega|\ge2\pi B$, which differs only at the single frequency $2\pi B$ itself). The exams say "bandlimited to $20$ kHz", "bandlimited to $\Omega_{\max}=1000\pi$ rad/s", or draw $X_c(\Omega)$ with its edges: in every case $B$ is the edge in hertz, $B=\Omega_{\max}/2\pi$.

The central copy of a bandlimited spectrum occupies $|\Omega|\le2\pi B$; the next one, centred at $2\pi/T$, occupies $\frac{2\pi}{T}-2\pi B\le\Omega\le\frac{2\pi}{T}+2\pi B$. They stay apart when the right edge of the first is to the left of the left edge of the second (notes eqs. 18–20, inked on slide 8):

$$
\underbrace{2\pi B}_{\text{right edge of the }n=0\text{ copy}}\ \lt\ \underbrace{\frac{2\pi}{T}-2\pi B}_{\text{left edge of the }n=1\text{ copy}} .
$$

Move the $2\pi B$ across: $4\pi B\lt\dfrac{2\pi}{T}$, i.e. $2B\lt\dfrac1T$, i.e. $f_s\gt2B$.

> [!key] Nyquist criterion and Nyquist rate (slide 9)
> A signal bandlimited to $B$ hertz can be sampled without overlapping spectral copies — and, as the next lectures show, recovered from its samples — if
> $$
> f_s\gt2B\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad T\lt\frac{1}{2B}.
> $$
> The threshold $f_{\text{Nyquist}}=2B$ is the **Nyquist rate** (hertz) and $\frac{1}{2B}$ the corresponding period, which the past exams call the **Nyquist period** or **Nyquist interval** $T_N$ (also written $T_{NYQ}$). Three equivalent readings: the sampling rate exceeds twice the highest frequency; the band edge $2\pi B$ lies below half the sampling frequency in rad/s, $\pi/T$; on the $\omega$ axis the band edge $2\pi BT$ lies inside the period, $2\pi BT\lt\pi$.

The three panels of the notes' Figure 3, redrawn above, are the three regimes:

- **Above the Nyquist rate** ($f_s\gt2B$): the copies are separated by a **guard band**, the empty interval between $2\pi B$ and $\frac{2\pi}{T}-2\pi B$ (in $\omega$: between $2\pi BT$ and $2\pi-2\pi BT$). The central copy is an untouched, rescaled $X_a$.
- **Exactly at the Nyquist rate** ($f_s=2B$): no guard band. The copies **touch at their edges** and do not overlap. Example 1(b) is this case: the boxes merge into a flat line, which looks dramatic, but each copy is still intact on its own interval.
- **Below the Nyquist rate** ($f_s\lt2B$): the copies **overlap and add**. Their sum has a new shape — in the figure, flat where two triangles overlap — and the original spectrum cannot be read off any more. This is **aliasing**; its consequences (which frequencies land where, and what comes back out of a D/A converter) are the subject of the next lectures.

## 7. Example 2: sampling around the Nyquist rate

> [!question] Slides 10–12 — a triangle at three sampling rates
> Let
> $$
> X_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{|\Omega|}{100\pi}, & |\Omega|\le100\pi\\[4pt] 0, & |\Omega|\gt100\pi\end{cases}
> $$
> Sketch $X_d(\omega)$ for (a) $T=\frac{1}{150}$ s, (b) $T=\frac{1}{100}$ s, (c) $T=\frac{1}{75}$ s.

Before the cases: the band edge is $100\pi$ rad/s, so $B=50$ Hz and the Nyquist rate is $100$ Hz. The three sampling frequencies, $150$, $100$ and $75$ Hz, are above, at and below it — the example is the notes' Figure 3 with numbers. In each case $X_d$ is the sum of triangles of height $\frac1T$ and half-width $100\pi T$, centred at the multiples of $2\pi$. (The slides are not inked for this example; the answers below are worked out here and checked numerically, both by summing copies and by taking the DTFT of the actual samples $x[n]=x_a(nT)$ of $x_a(t)=50\,\frac{\sin^2(50\pi t)}{(50\pi t)^2}$, the inverse CTFT of the triangle — see the Python in §10.)

> [!success]- Answer (a) $T = 1/150$ s — above the Nyquist rate (checked)
> Height $\frac1T=150$; half-width $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{150}=\frac{2\pi}{3}$. The central copy is
> $$
> X_d(\omega)=150\left(1-\frac{|\omega|}{2\pi/3}\right)=150-\frac{225}{\pi}|\omega|\qquad\text{for }|\omega|\le\frac{2\pi}{3},
> $$
> so $X_d(0)=150$, $X_d\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=75$, $X_d\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=0$. The $n=1$ copy starts at $2\pi-\frac{2\pi}{3}=\frac{4\pi}{3}$: there is a **guard band** $\frac{2\pi}{3}\lt|\omega|\lt\frac{4\pi}{3}$ where $X_d=0$, and on the period $[-\pi,\pi]$ the DTFT is one triangle with zero on $\frac{2\pi}{3}\lt|\omega|\le\pi$.

> [!success]- Answer (b) $T = 1/100$ s — exactly at the Nyquist rate (checked)
> Height $100$; half-width $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{100}=\pi$. The central copy fills the whole period,
> $$
> X_d(\omega)=100\left(1-\frac{|\omega|}{\pi}\right)\qquad\text{for }|\omega|\le\pi,
> $$
> with $X_d(0)=100$, $X_d\!\left(\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=50$, $X_d(\pm\pi)=0$. The copy centred at $2\pi$ starts at exactly $\pi$, where the central one ends: the triangles **touch** at $\pm\pi,\pm3\pi,\dots$ and the DTFT is a continuous zigzag with no flat parts and no guard band. Nothing overlaps, so each copy is still the complete $X_a$.

> [!success]- Answer (c) $T = 1/75$ s — below the Nyquist rate (checked)
> Height $75$; half-width $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{75}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\gt\pi$. Each copy spills past the edge of the period, and on $\frac{2\pi}{3}\le\omega\le\pi$ two copies are nonzero: the central one, $75\left(1-\frac{\omega}{4\pi/3}\right)$, and the one centred at $2\pi$, $75\left(1-\frac{2\pi-\omega}{4\pi/3}\right)$. Their sum is
> $$
> 75\left(2-\frac{\omega+(2\pi-\omega)}{4\pi/3}\right)=75\left(2-\frac{2\pi}{4\pi/3}\right)=75\left(2-\frac32\right)=37.5 ,
> $$
> a **constant**: the falling edge of one triangle and the rising edge of the next have opposite slopes and cancel. So on one period
> $$
> X_d(\omega)=\begin{cases}75\left(1-\dfrac{3|\omega|}{4\pi}\right), & |\omega|\le\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\\[6pt] 37.5, & \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\le|\omega|\le\pi\end{cases}
> $$
> continuous at $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ (the central copy alone is already $75\cdot\frac12=37.5$ there) and flat out to $\pm\pi$ and beyond, to $\pm\frac{4\pi}{3}$, where the next single-copy region begins. The DTFT is a trapezoid, not a triangle: the overlap has changed the shape, and from $X_d$ alone you could not tell that $X_a$ was a triangle reaching $100\pi$. That is aliasing. (At $\omega=\pi$ each copy contributes $75\left(1-\frac34\right)=18.75$.)

## 8. Sampling a sinusoid

The relation $\omega=\Omega T$ was found by sampling a cosine, and the cosine is worth a second look because it shows what aliasing does one frequency at a time. Sampling $x_a(t)=\cos(\Omega_0t)$ gives $x[n]=\cos(\omega_0n)$ with $\omega_0=\Omega_0T$; in the frequency domain the CTFT $\pi[\delta(\Omega-\Omega_0)+\delta(\Omega+\Omega_0)]$ becomes, through the copies formula, $\pi[\delta(\omega-\omega_0)+\delta(\omega+\omega_0)]$ repeated every $2\pi$ — the DTFT pair of [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]], and a consistency check on the $\frac1T$: the copy $\frac1T\,\pi\,\delta\!\left(\frac{\omega-2\pi n}{T}-\Omega_0\right)$ is $\pi\,\delta(\omega-2\pi n-\omega_0)$, area $\pi$ as in the DTFT pair, because $\delta(u/T)=T\,\delta(u)$ cancels the $\frac1T$.

Now the catch. A DTFT is $2\pi$-periodic, so $\cos(\omega_0n)$ and $\cos\big((\omega_0+2\pi k)n\big)$ are the **same sequence** for every integer $k$, and since the cosine is even, so is $\cos\big((-\omega_0+2\pi k)n\big)$. Two analog sinusoids therefore produce identical samples whenever

$$
\Omega_0'\,T\equiv\pm\,\Omega_0\,T\pmod{2\pi},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\Omega_0'=\pm\Omega_0+\frac{2\pi}{T}k=\pm\Omega_0+2\pi f_s\,k .
$$

Adding any multiple of the sampling frequency to the analog frequency, or negating it, leaves the samples unchanged. This is the seed of aliasing: once sampled, a sinusoid remembers its frequency only modulo $f_s$ (and up to sign). The Nyquist criterion for a single tone, $f_s\gt2f_0$, is exactly the condition $0\le\omega_0\lt\pi$ that makes the digital frequency unambiguous within one period.

## 9. Traps

> [!trap] Where points go on sampling questions
> - **The height $1/T$.** Every copy is scaled: a triangle of height $1$ sampled at $1$ kHz has height $1000$ in $X_d$. Every key labels it ($1000$ and $500$ in [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #5(b), $5000$ in [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2]] #8(b)); a sketch without it is a different spectrum.
> - **Spacing: $2\pi/T$ in $\Omega$, $2\pi$ in $\omega$.** $X_s(\Omega)$ repeats every $2\pi/T$ rad/s; $X_d(\omega)$ repeats every $2\pi$ rad/sample. Mixing the two axes is a labelling slip in the FA2024 key, whose $X_d$ sketches are headed "$X_d(\Omega)$".
> - **"Bandlimited to $B$ Hz" means $|\Omega|\le2\pi B$.** Convert before anything else; the Nyquist rate is $2B$ in hertz, $4\pi B$ in rad/s. For $\Omega_{\max}=1000\pi$ rad/s the rate is $1000$ Hz, not $2000\pi$.
> - **Nyquist is strict:** $f_s\gt2B$. At exactly $2B$ the copies touch; the spectrum is not overlapped, but there is no guard band, and a sinusoid at $B$ itself sampled at $2B$ gives $\cos(\pi n)=(-1)^n$ or $\sin(\pi n)=0$ — which is why [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(b) rejects $T=\frac{1}{40}$ s for a $20$ Hz sine.
> - **Aliasing is the overlap, not the copies.** The copies are always there; they are harmless while they stay apart. Only when they add does $X_d$ stop being a scaled replica of $X_a$. And when they do overlap, draw the *sum*: pieces of lines add to lines, and two triangles can add to a flat segment.
> - **$T_N$ versus the Nyquist rate.** $T_N=\frac{1}{2B}$ is a period in seconds, $f_{\text{Nyquist}}=2B$ a rate in hertz; "find the Nyquist interval" wants the former ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2019|FA2019 MT2]] #9(a)), "the largest sampling period such that no aliasing occurs" too ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #5(a)).
> - **Increasing $T$ stretches $X_d$, it does not shrink it.** A fixed $\Omega$ lands at $\omega=\Omega T$, farther from $0$ for larger $T$; only the height $\frac1T$ shrinks ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #1(e), False).
> - **Only bandlimited signals have a Nyquist rate.** A signal with energy at arbitrarily high frequencies aliases at every sampling rate ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] #1(d), False).

## 10. In Python

Example 2 by both routes: the copies formula, and the DTFT of the actual samples $x[n]=x_a(nT)$, where $x_a(t)=50\,\operatorname{sinc}^2(50t)$ (numpy's $\operatorname{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u}$) is the inverse CTFT of the triangle. The DTFT sum is truncated at $|n|\le4000$; because $x_a$ decays like $1/t^2$ the truncation error is about $0.01$.

```python
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(suppress=True)

# Example 2: X_a(W) = 1 - |W|/100pi on |W| <= 100pi  (B = 50 Hz, Nyquist rate 100 Hz)
Xa = lambda W: np.clip(1 - np.abs(W) / (100 * np.pi), 0, None)

def Xd_copies(w, T):                     # X_d(w) = (1/T) sum_n X_a((w - 2 pi n)/T)
    return sum(Xa((w - 2 * np.pi * n) / T) for n in range(-5, 6)) / T

def Xd_samples(w, T, N=4000):            # the DTFT of the actual samples x[n] = x_a(nT), x_a(t) = 50 sinc^2(50 t)
    n = np.arange(-N, N + 1)
    x = 50 * np.sinc(50 * n * T) ** 2    # inverse CTFT of the triangle (numpy's sinc is sin(pi u)/(pi u))
    return np.sum(x * np.exp(-1j * w * n)).real

w = np.array([0, 2 * np.pi / 3, 5 * np.pi / 6, np.pi])
for T in (1 / 150, 1 / 100, 1 / 75):
    print(f"T = 1/{round(1/T)} s  X_d at w = 0, 2pi/3, 5pi/6, pi:",
          np.round(Xd_copies(w, T), 2), " from the samples:", np.round([Xd_samples(wi, T) for wi in w], 2))
```

```text
T = 1/150 s  X_d at w = 0, 2pi/3, 5pi/6, pi: [150.   0.   0.   0.]  from the samples: [149.99   0.01  -0.     0.  ]
T = 1/100 s  X_d at w = 0, 2pi/3, 5pi/6, pi: [100.    33.33  16.67   0.  ]  from the samples: [99.99 33.33 16.67  0.01]
T = 1/75 s  X_d at w = 0, 2pi/3, 5pi/6, pi: [75.  37.5 37.5 37.5]  from the samples: [75.  37.5 37.5 37.5]
```

The two routes agree: the heights $150$, $100$, $75$; the guard band at $T=\frac{1}{150}$ s (zero from $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ on); the touching triangles at $T=\frac{1}{100}$ s ($100(1-\frac{2}{3})=33.33$, $100(1-\frac56)=16.67$, $0$ at $\pi$); and the flat $37.5$ from $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ to $\pi$ at $T=\frac{1}{75}$ s. Sampling in time really does add shifted copies in frequency.
