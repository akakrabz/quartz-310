*Lecture 19 · Wed Oct 14, 2026 · notes + slides "Aliasing effect" · prev: [[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/18-ideal-digital-to-analog-conversion|Lecture 18]] · next: Lecture 20 (to come)*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Sampling sends every analog frequency $\Omega$ to the digital frequency $\omega=\Omega T$, and a DTFT holds all of its information in one period, $[-\pi,\pi]$. A signal bandlimited to $B$ hertz keeps all its frequencies inside that period when its highest one does, $2\pi BT\lt\pi$: the **Nyquist criterion** $f_s\gt2B$ again, now read off the frequency axis. At exactly $f_s=2B$ the band edge lands on $\pm\pi$, which is safe when the spectrum vanishes there (the notes' condition $X_d(\pm\pi)=0$) and fails for a sinusoid at exactly $B$ hertz: $\sin(\Omega_0t)$ sampled at $f_s=\Omega_0/\pi$ gives $\sin(\pi n)=0$ for every $n$. Below the Nyquist rate the spectral copies overlap, the period $[-\pi,\pi]$ holds a spectrum of a different shape, and an ideal D/A converter rebuilds a different signal. That is **aliasing**, and nothing done to the samples afterwards can undo it. For a sinusoid the effect is exact: $\sin(\Omega_0t)$ becomes $\sin(\omega_0n)$ with $\omega_0=\Omega_0T$; every $\omega_0+2\pi k$ (and, for a cosine, every $-\omega_0+2\pi k$) gives the same samples; the D/A returns the one in $(-\pi,\pi]$, the **principal alias** $\hat\omega$, as the analog frequency $\hat\Omega=\hat\omega/T$. The lecture's $\sin(100\pi t)$ comes back as itself at $160$ and $120$ Hz, as $-\sin(60\pi t)$ at $80$ Hz and as $\sin(20\pi t)$ at $40$ Hz. In hertz: a tone at $f_0$ comes back at $f_0-kf_s$, whichever of these is closest to zero.

## 1. The Nyquist criterion, read off the ω axis

The last two lectures built the two converters. The ideal A/D ([[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/17-ideal-analog-to-digital-conversion|Lecture 17]]) takes $x[n]=x_a(nT)$, and its DTFT is the analog spectrum copied every $2\pi$ and scaled by $\frac1T$:

$$
X_d(\omega)=\frac1T\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X_a\!\left(\frac{\omega-2\pi k}{T}\right),\qquad\omega=\Omega T .
$$

The ideal D/A ([[4-sampling-and-reconstruction/18-ideal-digital-to-analog-conversion|Lecture 18]]) keeps the one period $|\omega|\le\pi$, relabels it $\Omega=\omega/T$ and multiplies by $T$: it builds the signal whose spectrum is $T\,X_d(\Omega T)$ for $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$ and zero beyond. Both lectures assumed a bandlimited signal sampled above the Nyquist rate, so that the round trip returns $x_a(t)$. Today is about what happens when it does not. (The past exams write the analog signal $x_c(t)$ with spectrum $X_c(\Omega)$, the Nyquist period $T_N$, and the converters "C/D" and "D/C"; they are the same objects.)

A signal is **bandlimited** to $B$ hertz if (notes eq. 1)

$$
X_a(\Omega)=0,\qquad|\Omega|\gt2\pi B ,
$$

$B$ being the largest linear frequency present. Lecture 17 derived the Nyquist criterion from the edges of neighbouring copies. The notes now give a second derivation, from $\omega=\Omega T$ alone (eqs. 2–5). A DTFT is $2\pi$-periodic, so everything $x[n]$ knows sits in $\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]$. For nothing to be lost, every frequency present in $X_a(\Omega)$ should land in that interval, and it is enough to check the largest one, $\Omega_{\max}=2\pi B$, which lands at $2\pi BT$:

$$
2\pi BT\lt\pi\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad T\lt\frac{1}{2B}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f_s\gt2B .
$$

> [!key] Nyquist criterion (slide 3)
> A bandlimited continuous-time signal with maximum linear frequency $B$ (hertz) can be sampled with period $T$ to produce $x[n]$ such that $x_a(t)$ may be recovered from $x[n]$ if
> $$
> T\lt\frac{1}{2B}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f_s\gt2B .
> $$
> Otherwise we incur **aliasing**. The **Nyquist rate** is $f_{\text{nyquist}}=2B$, in hertz. On the $\omega$ axis: the band edge $2\pi BT$ must lie inside $(-\pi,\pi)$.

The two derivations describe the same inequality. In Lecture 17 the copy centred at $2\pi$ starts at $2\pi-2\pi BT$, and it stays clear of the central copy, which ends at $2\pi BT$, exactly when $2\pi BT\lt\pi$. Lecture 17 asked *do the copies overlap?*; today's version asks *does every analog frequency get a digital frequency of its own?* The second question is the one that extends to single sinusoids (§4), where a "copy" is just a pair of arrows.

The band edge $2\pi B$ lands at $2\pi BT=1.4\pi$, outside the period, and the copy centred at $-2\pi$ brings the same frequency in at $1.4\pi-2\pi=-0.6\pi$, inside it. In the overlap region each digital frequency now carries two analog frequencies at once, and their contributions add.

## 2. Can we sample at exactly the Nyquist rate?

At $f_s=2B$ the band edge lands on $\pm\pi$, and the copy centred at $2\pi$ begins exactly where the central one ends. The notes' answer: "we may even be able to sample at the Nyquist rate when $X_d(\pm\pi)=0$", as for the triangle of panel (c). A triangle is zero at its own edge, so the meeting point carries nothing and nothing is lost. HW7 #4(b) is this case: a triangle reaching $50\pi$ rad/s ($B=25$ Hz) sampled at $50$ Hz gives $X_d(\omega)=50\left(1-\frac{|\omega|}{\pi}\right)$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$, zero at $\pm\pi$, and the ideal D/A, which multiplies by $T=\frac{1}{50}$ and relabels $\Omega=50\,\omega$, returns $X_a$ exactly (checked on a grid; the [[exams/quiz-2|Quiz 2 page]] works the whole problem).

The opposite extreme is a spectrum that is *all* edge: a sinusoid. Slide 7 takes $x_a(t)=\sin(\Omega_0t)$, for which $B=\frac{\Omega_0}{2\pi}$, and samples it at exactly $f_s=2B=\frac{\Omega_0}{\pi}$ (hertz; the slide's "seconds" is a slip), so $T=\frac{\pi}{\Omega_0}$. Then (notes eqs. 9–11, inked on slide 7)

$$
x[n]=x_a(nT)=\sin(\Omega_0nT)=\sin\!\left(\Omega_0n\frac{\pi}{\Omega_0}\right)=\sin(\pi n)=0\qquad\text{for all }n .
$$

Every sample lands on a zero crossing of the sine. A sequence of zeros could have come from $\sin(\Omega_0t)$, from $5\sin(\Omega_0t)$, from $x_a(t)=0$: there is no way to know what was sampled. In the frequency domain, $\sin(\omega_0n)\leftrightarrow-j\pi[\delta(\omega-\omega_0)-\delta(\omega+\omega_0)]$ with $\omega_0=\pi$ puts an arrow $-j\pi$ at $\omega=\pi$, and the copy centred at $2\pi$ puts its arrow $+j\pi$ (from $-\pi+2\pi$) on the same spot. They cancel, and $X_d(\omega)=0$ everywhere.

> [!note] The cosine survives, but only by luck of phase
> Sample $\cos(\Omega_0t)$ at the same rate and $x[n]=\cos(\pi n)=(-1)^n$: not zero, and the two arrows at $\omega=\pi$ now add instead of cancelling. The sinc interpolation of Lecture 18 even returns $\cos(\Omega_0t)$ from $(-1)^n$ (checked with symmetric partial sums of $\sum_n(-1)^n\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-nT)}{T}\right)$, with the course's $\mathrm{sinc}(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t}$). But give the cosine a phase: $\cos(\Omega_0t+\phi)$ samples to $\cos(\pi n+\phi)=\cos\phi\,(-1)^n$, so amplitude and phase collapse into the single number $\cos\phi$, and the D/A returns $\cos\phi\cdot\cos(\Omega_0t)$. The sine is the case $\phi=-\frac{\pi}{2}$, where that number is $0$. Because a sinusoid at exactly $B$ hertz cannot be recovered for every phase, the criterion is strict, $f_s\gt2B$.

The notes' condition $X_d(\pm\pi)=0$ is the safe one to quote. It is sufficient, not necessary. For an ordinary spectrum without impulses, the single frequency $\pm\pi$ carries no energy, so copies that touch at a nonzero value still lose nothing: the box of Lecture 17's Example 1(b) samples to $10\,\delta[n]$, whose sinc interpolation is the original signal exactly, and the HW7 #3(c) key calls the V-shaped spectrum at its Nyquist rate "touching at edges but not overlapping". What fails is an **impulse** at the band edge, a spectral line of nonzero area sitting on $\pm\pi$. That is exactly what a sinusoid at $B$ hertz contributes, and it is why the criterion is stated strictly.

## 3. What aliasing is

Below the Nyquist rate the copies overlap (Figure 1(d)). The shape of $X_a(\Omega)$ is no longer represented in $[-\pi,\pi]$, so ideal D/A conversion cannot return $x_a(t)$: it returns a corrupted signal whose frequencies have been moved and mixed.

> [!key] Aliasing (slide 8, notes §2)
> Bandlimited analog signals sampled **above** the Nyquist rate can be recovered perfectly from $x[n]$ by ideal D/A conversion. Sampled **below** the Nyquist rate, the sampled signal $x[n]$ takes on an incorrect, alternative appearance, an **alias**: it no longer expresses the original signal, and $x[n]$ is said to exhibit **aliasing**. Once a signal is aliased, its original representation cannot be recovered.

> [!intuition] Why "alias"
> An alias is a false name. A frequency above half the sampling rate cannot appear in $[-\pi,\pi]$ under its own name, so it turns up under the name of a lower one. Singer and Munson put it as frequencies that "masquerade in a band to which they do not belong". The samples carry no label saying which frequency produced them, and that is why aliasing cannot be undone: two different analog signals with identical samples are, as far as anything downstream of the A/D can tell, the same signal.

The notes add that aliasing of a general signal is "difficult to quantify and compute". You add up overlapping copies, as in Lecture 17's Example 2(c), and §8 comes back to that. For **sinusoids** it is exact and easy, so the lecture works with them.

## 4. Aliasing of sinusoids

Sample $x_a(t)=\sin(\Omega_0t)$ (notes eqs. 6–8):

$$
x[n]=\sin(\Omega_0nT)=\sin(\omega_0n),\qquad\omega_0=\Omega_0T .
$$

The signal is bandlimited with $B=\frac{\Omega_0}{2\pi}$, so by the criterion it aliases when $T\gt\frac{1}{2B}=\frac{\pi}{\Omega_0}$, that is, when $\omega_0\gt\pi$. What does it alias *to*? Since $n$ is an integer, adding $2\pi$ to the frequency changes nothing:

$$
\sin\big((\omega_0+2\pi k)n\big)=\sin(\omega_0n+2\pi kn)=\sin(\omega_0n)\qquad\text{for every integer }k .
$$

And for a cosine one more family gives the same samples, because the cosine is even: $\cos(-\omega_0n)=\cos(\omega_0n)$. For a sine the same move flips the sign, $\sin(-\omega_0n)=-\sin(\omega_0n)$.

> [!key] Which analog frequencies give the same samples
> At sampling period $T$, two sinusoids of frequencies $\Omega_0$ and $\Omega_1$ give identical samples when
> $$
> \cos:\quad\Omega_1T\equiv\pm\,\Omega_0T\pmod{2\pi},\qquad\qquad\sin:\quad\Omega_1T\equiv+\,\Omega_0T\pmod{2\pi},
> $$
> i.e. $\Omega_1=\pm\Omega_0+\frac{2\pi}{T}k$ for a cosine and $\Omega_1=\Omega_0+\frac{2\pi}{T}k$ for a sine (with $-\Omega_0+\frac{2\pi}{T}k$ a sine gives the negated sequence). A cosine with a phase, $\cos(\Omega_0t+\phi)$, behaves like the sine: $-\Omega_0$ would turn $+\phi$ into $-\phi$.

Out of all these frequencies the ideal D/A must pick one. It keeps only $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$, i.e. $|\omega|\le\pi$, so it picks the representative of $\omega_0$ in one period:

> [!key] Principal alias and recovered frequency
> The **principal alias** of $\omega_0$ is the $\hat\omega\in(-\pi,\pi]$ with $\hat\omega=\omega_0-2\pi k$ for an integer $k$. Then $\sin(\omega_0n)=\sin(\hat\omega n)$, and ideal D/A conversion with the same $T$ returns
> $$
> \hat x(t)=\sin(\hat\Omega t),\qquad\hat\Omega=\frac{\hat\omega}{T}\ \text{rad/s},\qquad|\hat\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T},
> $$
> the only sinusoid of frequency at most $\frac{\pi}{T}$ through the samples. Without aliasing ($|\omega_0|\lt\pi$) $\hat\omega=\omega_0$ and $\hat\Omega=\Omega_0$. With aliasing $\hat\Omega\ne\Omega_0$, and $\hat\Omega$ may be negative: $\sin(\hat\Omega t)=-\sin(|\hat\Omega|t)$.

In the frequency domain the same thing is the DTFT pair the lecture recalls on slide 9 (notes eq. 13),

$$
\sin(\omega_0n)\ \overset{\mathcal F}{\longleftrightarrow}\ -j\pi\big(\delta(\omega-\omega_0)-\delta(\omega+\omega_0)\big),\qquad\text{repeated every }2\pi .
$$

When $\omega_0\gt\pi$, the central pair of arrows sits outside $[-\pi,\pi]$, and the arrows inside the period are the ones that the neighbouring copies have brought in. They sit at $\pm\hat\omega$, and those are all the D/A sees.

## 5. Example: sin(100πt) at four sampling rates

> [!question] Slides 9–13 — one tone, four sampling rates
> $x_a(t)=\sin(100\pi t)$: $B=50$ Hz, $\Omega_0=100\pi$ rad/s, Nyquist rate $100$ Hz. Sample it at (1) $f_s=160$ Hz (well above the Nyquist rate), (2) $120$ Hz (slightly above), (3) $80$ Hz (slightly below), (4) $40$ Hz (well below). For each, find the digital frequency $\omega_k=\Omega_0T_k$, its principal alias, $x_k[n]$, the recovered frequency $\hat\Omega_k$ after ideal D/A conversion with the same $T$, and the recovered signal $\hat x_k(t)$.

> [!success]- Answer (notes eqs. 14–17; checked sample by sample and by sinc interpolation)
> | $f_s$ | $\omega_k=100\pi T_k$ | principal alias $\hat\omega_k$ | $x_k[n]$ | $\hat\Omega_k=\hat\omega_k/T_k$ | $\hat x_k(t)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $160$ Hz | $\frac{5\pi}{8}$ | $\frac{5\pi}{8}$ | $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{8}n\right)$ | $100\pi$ | $\sin(100\pi t)$ |
> | $120$ Hz | $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ | $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ | $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}n\right)$ | $100\pi$ | $\sin(100\pi t)$ |
> | $80$ Hz | $\frac{5\pi}{4}$ | $-\frac{3\pi}{4}$ | $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{4}n\right)=\sin\!\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right)$ | $-60\pi$ | $\sin(-60\pi t)=-\sin(60\pi t)$ |
> | $40$ Hz | $\frac{5\pi}{2}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{2}n\right)=\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}n\right)$ | $20\pi$ | $\sin(20\pi t)$ |
>
> **(1)–(2)** $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{160}=\frac{5\pi}{8}$ and $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{120}=\frac{5\pi}{6}$ are already inside $(-\pi,\pi)$: no aliasing, $\hat\Omega=\frac{5\pi/8}{1/160}=100\pi$ and $\frac{5\pi/6}{1/120}=100\pi$. The D/A returns the original 50 Hz sine.
>
> **(3)** $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{80}=\frac{5\pi}{4}\gt\pi$. Subtract $2\pi$: $\hat\omega=\frac{5\pi}{4}-2\pi=-\frac{3\pi}{4}$, and $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{4}n\right)=\sin\!\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right)=-\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right)$. The D/A returns $\hat\Omega=-\frac{3\pi}{4}\cdot80=-60\pi$ rad/s: $\hat x(t)=\sin(-60\pi t)=-\sin(60\pi t)$, a **30 Hz** sine with its sign flipped. In the DTFT, the arrow inside the period at $-\frac{3\pi}{4}$ is the $+\omega_0$ arrow of the copy centred at $-2\pi$ ($-2\pi+\frac{5\pi}{4}$), carrying $-j\pi$; the one at $+\frac{3\pi}{4}$ is the $-\omega_0$ arrow of the copy at $2\pi$, carrying $+j\pi$. Read inside the window, that is $-j\pi\big(\delta(\omega-\hat\omega)-\delta(\omega+\hat\omega)\big)$ with $\hat\omega=-\frac{3\pi}{4}$: the transform of $\sin(\hat\omega n)$, sign and all.
>
> **(4)** $100\pi\cdot\frac{1}{40}=\frac{5\pi}{2}$. Subtract $2\pi$: $\hat\omega=\frac{\pi}{2}$. The D/A returns $\hat\Omega=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot40=20\pi$ rad/s: $\hat x(t)=\sin(20\pi t)$, a **10 Hz** sine, with the original sign this time. The arrows at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ come from the copies centred at $\mp2\pi$; the copies at $\pm4\pi$ are also in view, at $\pm\frac{3\pi}{2}$ ($4\pi-\frac{5\pi}{2}=\frac{3\pi}{2}$).
>
> In hertz the four answers are $50$, $50$, $-30$ and $10$ Hz, i.e. $50-k f_s$ with $k=0,0,1,1$.

## 6. The folding recipe

> [!recipe] Where does a sampled tone go?
> Given $x_a(t)=A\cos(\Omega_0t+\phi)$ or $A\sin(\Omega_0t)$ and the sampling period $T$ (rate $f_s=\frac1T$):
> 1. **Digital frequency:** $\omega_0=\Omega_0T=2\pi\frac{f_0}{f_s}$.
> 2. **Reduce it** into $(-\pi,\pi]$: $\hat\omega=\omega_0-2\pi k$, where $k$ is the integer nearest to $\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}=\frac{f_0}{f_s}$. Then $x[n]=A\cos(\hat\omega n+\phi)$ or $A\sin(\hat\omega n)$, the same samples.
> 3. **Recovered analog frequency:** $\hat\Omega=\frac{\hat\omega}{T}$ rad/s, i.e. $\hat f=f_0-kf_s$ Hz: **the alias of $f_0$ is the $f_0-kf_s$ closest to $0$**. The frequency you hear or see is $|\hat f|$, always between $0$ and $\frac{f_s}{2}$.
> 4. **Signs:** the D/A returns $A\cos(\hat\Omega t+\phi)$ or $A\sin(\hat\Omega t)$. If $\hat\Omega\lt0$, rewrite: $A\cos(|\hat\Omega|t-\phi)$ (the phase flips) or $-A\sin(|\hat\Omega|t)$ (the sign flips). A plain cosine does not care.
> 5. **Boundary:** if $\hat\omega=\pi$ exactly ($f_0$ an odd multiple of $\frac{f_s}{2}$), a sine samples to zeros and a cosine to $\cos\phi\,(-1)^n$ (§2).

The left panel is the reason for the word *folding*. Hold $f_s$ fixed and sweep the input frequency upward. The apparent frequency rises to $\frac{f_s}{2}$, falls back to $0$ at $f_s$, rises again, and so on, as if the frequency axis were a strip of paper folded at every multiple of $\frac{f_s}{2}$. Some books call $\frac{f_s}{2}$ the *folding frequency*; in this course it is half the sampling rate, and the Nyquist *rate* $2B$ is a property of the signal. The right panel holds the 50 Hz tone fixed and sweeps $f_s$: above $100$ Hz it comes back as itself, below it lands at $50-kf_s$.

> [!example] Three tones at $f_s=80$ Hz (checked)
> - $f_0=70$ Hz: $70-80=-10$ Hz. $\cos(140\pi t)$ comes back as $\cos(20\pi t)$; $\sin(140\pi t)$ as $-\sin(20\pi t)$.
> - $f_0=90$ Hz: $90-80=10$ Hz, positive, so $\sin(180\pi t)$ comes back as $+\sin(20\pi t)$.
> - $f_0=130$ Hz: $130-2\cdot80=-30$ Hz, the same alias as the lecture's 50 Hz tone.

## 7. Slide 19: which frequencies were sampled?

> [!question] Slide 19 — possible frequencies
> $x_a(t)=\cos(\Omega_0t)$ is sampled at $f_s=100$ Hz for some frequency $\Omega_0\in\mathbb R$, and the result is $x[n]=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}n\right)$. Determine two possible choices of $\Omega_0$. (Hint on the slide: remember to consider aliasing.)

> [!success]- Answer (the slide is not inked; worked out here and checked sample by sample)
> Sampling gives $x[n]=\cos\!\left(\frac{\Omega_0}{100}n\right)$. A cosine is even and the digital frequency counts only modulo $2\pi$, so we need
> $$
> \frac{\Omega_0}{100}\equiv\pm\frac{\pi}{3}\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\Omega_0=\pm\frac{100\pi}{3}+200\pi k .
> $$
> The positive ones are $\frac{100\pi}{3},\ \frac{500\pi}{3},\ \frac{700\pi}{3},\ \frac{1100\pi}{3},\dots$ rad/s, i.e. $16.7,\ 83.3,\ 116.7,\ 183.3,\dots$ Hz. Two choices: $\boldsymbol{\Omega_0=\frac{100\pi}{3}}$ **rad/s** (no aliasing: $\frac{\pi}{3}\lt\pi$) and $\boldsymbol{\Omega_0=\frac{500\pi}{3}}$ **rad/s**. Check the second: $\frac{500\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{100}=\frac{5\pi}{3}$, and $\cos\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{3}n\right)=\cos\!\left(2\pi n-\frac{\pi}{3}n\right)=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}n\right)$. (A brute-force scan of $0$–$200$ Hz finds exactly $16.67$, $83.33$, $116.67$ and $183.33$ Hz. The negative $\Omega_0=-\frac{100\pi}{3}$ also works, but it is the same signal, $\cos$ being even.)
>
> Two follow-ups. An ideal D/A at $100$ Hz would return only $\cos\!\left(\frac{100\pi}{3}t\right)$, the principal alias, whichever of these was sampled. If the slide had said $\sin(\Omega_0t)\to\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}n\right)$, only $\Omega_0=\frac{100\pi}{3}+200\pi k$ would do ($\frac{100\pi}{3}$, $\frac{700\pi}{3}$, …), because $\frac{500\pi}{3}$ gives $\sin\!\left(\frac{5\pi}{3}n\right)=-\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}n\right)$.

## 8. Aliasing of a whole spectrum

A general bandlimited signal is a continuum of sinusoids, and each one folds by the recipe of §6. In the language of Lecture 17, the overlapping copies **add**: on $[-\pi,\pi]$ the value of $X_d(\omega)$ collects $X_a$ at $\frac{\omega}{T}$ and also at $\frac{\omega-2\pi k}{T}$ for every other copy that reaches $\omega$. The ideal D/A then rebuilds the signal whose spectrum is $T\,X_d(\Omega T)$ on $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$:

$$
\hat X_a(\Omega)=\sum_{k}X_a\!\left(\Omega-\frac{2\pi k}{T}\right)\quad\text{for }|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T},\qquad\hat X_a(\Omega)=0\quad\text{for }|\Omega|\gt\frac{\pi}{T}.
$$

Without aliasing only the $k=0$ term reaches $|\Omega|\le\frac{\pi}{T}$ and $\hat X_a=X_a$, which is Lecture 18's perfect reconstruction. With aliasing the recovered spectrum is corrupted in two ways: it is missing everything above $\frac{\pi}{T}$, and below $\frac{\pi}{T}$ it carries the folded-back high frequencies on top of the true ones.

The figure finishes Lecture 17's Example 2(c). The triangle $X_a(\Omega)=1-\frac{|\Omega|}{100\pi}$ ($B=50$ Hz) sampled at $75$ Hz gave the trapezoid $X_d$, flat at $37.5$ for $\frac{2\pi}{3}\le|\omega|\le\pi$. Through the D/A ($\times T=\frac{1}{75}$, $\Omega=75\,\omega$):

$$
\hat X_a(\Omega)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{|\Omega|}{100\pi}, & |\Omega|\le50\pi\\[6pt] X_a(\Omega)+X_a(150\pi-|\Omega|)=\dfrac12, & 50\pi\le|\Omega|\le75\pi\\[6pt] 0, & |\Omega|\gt75\pi\end{cases}
$$

The band $75\pi$–$100\pi$ has been folded about $\frac{\pi}{T}=75\pi$ onto $50\pi$–$75\pi$. At $\Omega=60\pi$ the true spectrum is $0.4$ and the recovered one $0.5$ (checked); at $75\pi$, $0.25$ against $0.5$. From the samples alone the $0.5$ cannot be split back into its two parts. The past exams show how varied the results can look: copies that add to a constant ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2]] #8(b), [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #5(b)), copies of an odd spectrum that cancel to $X_d=0$ ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2023|SP2023 MT2]] #7(c), the same cancellation as the sine of §2), and a band-pass spectrum that lands upside down without any overlap at all ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #4, case 2: every frequency folds, so the band comes back mirrored).

> [!note] Aside — avoiding aliasing (not in Lecture 19's notes)
> The lecture defines aliasing and shows its effect; it does not discuss prevention, and the past keys do not ask for it. The two standard remedies follow from the criterion. **Sample faster**, so that $f_s\gt2B$. Or, when the signal is not truly bandlimited, put an **analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter** in front of the A/D to remove everything above $\frac{f_s}{2}$. That gives up those frequencies on purpose instead of letting them fold onto lower ones (Singer & Munson describe it as "very common", in the scanned ECE 410 notes on decimation, "Prevention of aliasing at A/D"). A different idea, which the exams *do* test, is to tolerate aliasing that a digital filter will delete anyway: see [[problems/digital-filtering-of-analog-signals|digital filtering of analog signals]].

## 9. Practice

> [!question] Practice 1 — which rates alias a 70 Hz tone?
> $x_a(t)=\cos(140\pi t)$ is sampled at $f_s=200,\ 150,\ 140,\ 120,\ 100,\ 60$ Hz and rebuilt by an ideal D/A at the same rate. Which rates alias it, and what frequency (in Hz) comes out for each?

> [!success]- Solution (checked sample by sample)
> The Nyquist rate is $140$ Hz. Fold $70-kf_s$ to the value closest to $0$:
> - $200$ Hz and $150$ Hz: above the Nyquist rate, no aliasing, out comes $70$ Hz.
> - $140$ Hz: exactly the Nyquist rate. $\omega_0=\pi$, $x[n]=(-1)^n$; this cosine (phase $0$) survives, but a sine would have given all zeros (§2). Not safe.
> - $120$ Hz: $70-120=-50$ Hz, so $\cos(100\pi t)$ comes out.
> - $100$ Hz: $70-100=-30$ Hz, so $\cos(60\pi t)$.
> - $60$ Hz: $70-60=10$ Hz, so $\cos(20\pi t)$.
>
> Aliased: $120$, $100$ and $60$ Hz (and $140$ Hz is the boundary).

> [!question] Practice 2 — signed aliases of a sine
> $x_a(t)=\sin(260\pi t)$ (130 Hz). Give the recovered signal $\hat x(t)$ after ideal A/D and D/A at (a) $300$ Hz, (b) $200$ Hz, (c) $100$ Hz, (d) $80$ Hz, (e) $50$ Hz.

> [!success]- Solution (checked sample by sample)
> (a) $300\gt260$: no aliasing, $\sin(260\pi t)$. (b) $130-200=-70$ Hz: $\sin(-140\pi t)=-\sin(140\pi t)$. (c) $130-100=30$ Hz: $\sin(60\pi t)$. (d) $130-2\cdot80=-30$ Hz: $-\sin(60\pi t)$. (e) $130-3\cdot50=-20$ Hz: $-\sin(40\pi t)$. Same 30 Hz in (c) and (d), opposite signs: the magnitude of the alias is not the whole answer for a sine.

> [!question] Practice 3 — the set of frequencies behind given samples
> At $f_s=50$ Hz a sinusoid gives $x[n]=\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}n\right)$. (a) Which of $\Omega_0=-80\pi,\ -20\pi,\ 20\pi,\ 80\pi,\ 120\pi,\ 180\pi$ rad/s are possible for $x_a(t)=\sin(\Omega_0t)$? (b) Which are possible if instead $x_a(t)=\cos(\Omega_0t)$ and $x[n]=\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}n\right)$?

> [!success]- Solution (checked sample by sample)
> $\omega_0=\frac{\Omega_0}{50}$. **(a)** A sine needs $\frac{\Omega_0}{50}\equiv+\frac{2\pi}{5}$, i.e. $\Omega_0=20\pi+100\pi k$: $\boldsymbol{-80\pi,\ 20\pi,\ 120\pi}$. The other three, $-20\pi$, $80\pi$ ($\frac{8\pi}{5}\equiv-\frac{2\pi}{5}$) and $180\pi$ ($\frac{18\pi}{5}\equiv-\frac{2\pi}{5}$), give $-\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}n\right)$. **(b)** A cosine accepts both signs, $\Omega_0=\pm20\pi+100\pi k$: **all six**.

> [!question] Practice 4 — two tones on one frequency
> Sample at $f_s=80$ Hz: (a) $x_a(t)=\cos(20\pi t)+\cos(140\pi t)$; (b) $x_a(t)=\sin(20\pi t)+\sin(140\pi t)$. Find $x[n]$ and the output of an ideal D/A in each case.

> [!success]- Solution (checked sample by sample)
> The 70 Hz tone folds to $70-80=-10$ Hz, onto the 10 Hz tone ($\frac{140\pi}{80}=\frac{7\pi}{4}\equiv-\frac{\pi}{4}$).
> **(a)** $\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)+\cos\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)=2\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)$; the D/A returns $2\cos(20\pi t)$. The samples cannot tell this input from $2\cos(20\pi t)$ or from $1.5\cos(20\pi t)+0.5\cos(140\pi t)$: only the sum of the two amplitudes survives.
> **(b)** $\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)+\sin\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}n\right)=0$ for every $n$: the aliased tone cancels the other one exactly, and the D/A returns $0$.

> [!question] Practice 5 — design the alias
> A 50 Hz tone goes through ideal A/D and D/A at rate $f_s$, with $20\lt f_s\le200$ Hz. (a) For $\cos(100\pi t)$, which $f_s$ make the output $\cos(20\pi t)$? (b) For $\sin(100\pi t)$, which $f_s$ make the output $+\sin(20\pi t)$?

> [!success]- Solution (checked by a scan of $f_s$ in steps of $0.01$ Hz)
> We need $50-kf_s=\pm10$ (cosine) or $+10$ (sine), with $|\hat f|=10\lt\frac{f_s}{2}$, i.e. $f_s\gt20$ Hz.
> **(a)** $50-kf_s=10$: $f_s=\frac{40}{k}=40$ Hz. $50-kf_s=-10$: $f_s=\frac{60}{k}=60$ or $30$ Hz. Answer: $\boldsymbol{f_s=30,\ 40,\ 60}$ **Hz** ($f_s=20$ Hz would put the tone on $\omega=5\pi\equiv\pi$, the boundary).
> **(b)** Only $+10$: $\boldsymbol{f_s=40}$ **Hz**. At $30$ and $60$ Hz the sine comes out as $-\sin(20\pi t)$. This is the reasoning of [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(c).

## 10. Traps

> [!trap] Where points go on aliasing questions
> - **The sine's sign.** $\sin(\hat\omega n)$ with $\hat\omega\lt0$ is $-\sin(|\hat\omega|n)$: the lecture's $80$ Hz case returns $-\sin(60\pi t)$, not $\sin(60\pi t)$. For a sine only $\Omega_0T\equiv+\omega_0$ works: [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(c) needs the $50$ Hz tone to come back as $+5$ Hz, so $f_s=45$ Hz; $f_s=55$ Hz would give $-5$ Hz, i.e. $-\sin(10\pi t)$. A cosine with a phase is just as sign-sensitive ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2023|SP2023 MT2]] #8(a)).
> - **A cosine at exactly the Nyquist rate.** It survives as $(-1)^n$, so it looks safe; a sine at the same rate gives zeros, and any phase scales the result by $\cos\phi$. The criterion is strict, $f_s\gt2B$; [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(b) rejects $T=\frac{1}{40}$ s for a 20 Hz sine.
> - **Hz versus rad/s.** $\hat\Omega=\hat\omega/T$ is in rad/s; $\hat f=\hat\omega/(2\pi T)$ in hertz. The $80$ Hz case gives $\hat\Omega=-60\pi$ rad/s, which is $-30$ Hz, not "$60\pi$ Hz". The folding rule $f_0-kf_s$ works in hertz with $f_s$, or in rad/s with $\frac{2\pi}{T}$; do not mix them.
> - **"Aliasing can be undone" is false.** The samples of $\sin(100\pi t)$ and $-\sin(60\pi t)$ at $80$ Hz are identical numbers, so no processing of $x[n]$ can tell them apart. Prior knowledge can narrow the choice (a band limit in [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2]] #9(b) leaves two candidates), but it cannot pick one of them.
> - **"Consistent" is not "unaliased".** A question asking which $T$ or $\Omega_0$ produce given samples accepts aliased answers ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(a) iv, $T=\frac{1}{40}$ s). A question about the D/A *output* is different: the output is the principal alias, so getting the input back requires no aliasing ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #6(b)).
> - **Multiples of $\pi$.** If $\Omega_0T$ is a multiple of $\pi$, a sine samples to zeros ($\sin(m\pi n)=0$): four of the six options of [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2]] #3.
> - **The D/A returns one frequency, not the input.** Reading $x[n]$ off a D/A output means sampling it, $x[n]=y(nT)$ ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2]] #9(a)), and the inputs that could have produced it are all the frequencies that fold onto it.

## 11. In Python

Two different analog frequencies giving identical samples, the folding rule in hertz, and slide 19 by brute force:

```python
import numpy as np

# Lecture 19, scenario 3: sin(100 pi t) (50 Hz) and -sin(60 pi t) (30 Hz) sampled at 80 Hz
fs = 80
n = np.arange(8)
x50 = np.sin(100 * np.pi * n / fs)
x30 = -np.sin(60 * np.pi * n / fs)
print("50 Hz tone: ", np.round(x50, 4) + 0.0)
print("-(30 Hz):   ", np.round(x30, 4) + 0.0)          # + 0.0 turns -0.0 into 0.0
m = np.arange(10**5)
print(f"largest difference over 10^5 samples: {np.max(np.abs(np.sin(100 * np.pi * m / fs) + np.sin(60 * np.pi * m / fs))):.0e}")

# the folding rule in hertz: the alias of f0 is f0 - k fs, the one closest to 0
def alias_hz(f0, fs):
    return f0 - fs * np.round(f0 / fs)       # not meant for the boundary f0 = fs/2 + k fs

for fs in (160, 120, 80, 40):
    print(f"fs = {fs:3d} Hz: 50 Hz comes back as {alias_hz(50, fs):5.1f} Hz")

# slide 19: which of these give cos(pi n / 3) at 100 Hz?
n = np.arange(1000)
for W0 in (100 * np.pi / 3, 200 * np.pi / 3, 500 * np.pi / 3, 700 * np.pi / 3):
    print(f"W0 = {W0 / np.pi * 3:4.0f}pi/3 rad/s:", np.allclose(np.cos(W0 * n / 100), np.cos(np.pi * n / 3)))
```
