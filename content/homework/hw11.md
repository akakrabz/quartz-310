## Problem 1 — which filter, and where are its edges?

> [!question] Problem 1
> For each of the following scenarios, decide which digital filter type (low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, or band-stop) should be used. Specify the cutoff frequencies for the passband(s) and stopband(s) of each filter allowing for the largest possible transition band. State all cutoff frequencies as digital radial frequencies between $0\le\omega\le\pi$.
>
> (a) A piece of music has been recorded with high-pitch background noise present. We know the music was recorded with ideal sampling at 24 kHz, the maximum frequency of the music is 4 kHz, and the background noise has a minimum frequency of 8 kHz. Specify the digital filter to remove the noise from the music.
>
> (b) Three people with distinct voices are speaking at the same time. We know the first person speaks in the range of 50 Hz – 200 Hz, the second person speaks in the range of 300 Hz – 500 Hz, and the third person speaks in the range of 600 Hz – 800 Hz. We record the speakers by sampling all three speaking at the same time with an ideal A/D converter at sampling frequency 2 kHz. Specify the digital filter to remove the first and third speakers and only listen to the second speaker.

**What it practises.** Lecture 27's Example 1, which the instructor called practice for exactly this question.

**Method** ([[concepts/filter-specifications|filter specifications]], recipe in §3):
1. Sort the content into "keep" and "remove". Which side of the kept band has something to remove: above, below, or both?
2. Convert every frequency in the text with $\omega=\frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$. Check that all of them are below $\pi$.
3. The passband must cover everything you keep; each stopband must cover everything you remove. "Largest possible transition band" means each transition band uses the whole gap between a kept edge and a removed edge.
4. Write the bands as intervals inside $[0,\pi]$. A filter with content to remove on both sides has two stopbands and two transition bands.

**Parallel examples, worked:** Example 1(a), thunder below voices at 4 kHz, and Example 1(b), a 60 Hz hum at 480 Hz, both on [[concepts/filter-specifications|filter specifications]].

## Problem 2 — FIR or IIR?

> [!question] Problem 2
> For each of the below parts, the transfer function $H(z)$ is given for an LTI system. Determine if each system is FIR or IIR and provide brief justification for your reasoning.
>
> (a) $H(z)=z^2+2z-3z^{-1}+4z^{-2}$
>
> (b) $H(z)=\dfrac{1+\frac12z^{-1}}{1-\frac12z^{-1}}$
>
> (c) $H(z)=\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\frac13z^{-1}\right)\left(1-e^{j(\pi/2)}z^{-1}\right)}$
>
> (d) $H(z)=\dfrac{5z^{-3}-3z^{-4}}{1-\frac35z^{-1}}$

**What it practises.** Midterm 1 material, as he said in lecture ("that's also something we've covered actually back in our midterm one content"): the test on $H(z)$ from [[concepts/fir-and-iir|FIR and IIR]].

**Method:**
1. FIR means $h[n]$ has finitely many non-zero samples. A finite sum of powers of $z$ is exactly that: each term $c\,z^{-m}$ is one sample $c\,\delta[n-m]$, wherever it sits in time ([[concepts/z-transform-pairs|z-transform pairs]]).
2. For a ratio, factor the numerator and the denominator and cancel common factors first ([[concepts/pole-zero-cancellation|pole-zero cancellation]]). A pole away from $z=0$ that survives contributes a term like $p^nu[n]$, which never ends.
3. Justify with one line: either the finite list of samples, or the surviving pole.

Stability and causality are separate questions and are not what is asked.

**Parallel examples, worked:** the recursive moving average and the Lecture 9 test on [[concepts/fir-and-iir|FIR and IIR]]; [[homework/hw3|HW3]] #3a and [[homework/hw4|HW4]] #6.

## Problem 3 — from the LCCDE to both diagrams

> [!question] Problem 3
> Consider a causal LTI system implemented by the below LCCDE:
> $$
> y[n]=\frac14y[n-1]-y[n-2]+x[n]-4x[n-2].
> $$
> (a) Determine the transfer function $H(z)$ of this LTI system.
>
> (b) Sketch the Direct Form-I block diagram for this system.
>
> (c) Sketch the Direct Form-II block diagram for this system.

**What it practises.** [[concepts/direct-form-structures|Direct form I and II]], the "LCCDE → diagrams" half of the recipe, after an $H(z)$ of the kind found since Lecture 9.

**Method:**
1. Take the z-transform of both sides, collect $Y(z)$ on the left, divide ([[concepts/transfer-function|transfer function]]). The feedback coefficients change sign when they move into the denominator.
2. List the input coefficients $c_0,c_1,c_2$ and the feedback coefficients $b_1,b_2$ of $y[n]=\sum b_iy[n-i]+\sum c_jx[n-j]$. A missing term has coefficient 0: its branch is left out, but its delay stays if a later term needs it.
3. Direct form I: a delay line on $x$ with the $c_j$, then a delay line on $y$ with the $b_i$.
4. Direct form II: one delay line; $b_i$ on the input side, $c_j$ on the output side. Count the delays as a check: $\max(K,M-1)$.

**Parallel example, worked:** Example 2, $y[n]=y[n-2]-2y[n-3]+3x[n]-x[n-1]+2x[n-2]$, on [[concepts/direct-form-structures|direct form I and II]], which also has a missing term.

## Problem 4 — from a direct form II diagram to the equation

> [!question] Problem 4
> A block diagram gives the Direct Form-II implementation of a causal LTI system. In the assignment's figure: $x[n]$ enters an adder, followed by a gain 1 and then the top of a chain of three delays. On the left, gains $\frac12$, $1$ and $-3$ lead from the outputs of the first, second and third delay back to the input adder. On the right, a gain $\frac32$ leads from the top of the chain, and gains $-1$, $\frac13$ and $1$ lead from the outputs of the first, second and third delay, to the output adder that produces $y[n]$.
>
> (a) Determine the transfer function $H(z)$ for this system.
>
> (b) Determine the LCCDE that describes this system.

**What it practises.** The other half of the recipe: "direct form II diagram → $H(z)$ → LCCDE".

**Method:**
1. Name the signal at the top of the delay chain $v[n]$. The three delay outputs are $v[n-1]$, $v[n-2]$, $v[n-3]$.
2. Write the input adder: $v[n]=x[n]+(\text{left gains})\times(\text{delayed }v)$. Transform and solve for $X(z)$ in terms of $V(z)$.
3. Write the output adder: $y[n]=(\text{right gains})\times v$ and its delays. Transform.
4. Divide $Y(z)$ by $X(z)$; $V(z)$ cancels. Then cross-multiply and return to the time domain for the LCCDE.
5. Check: the left-hand gains must reappear as the coefficients of $y[n-1]$, $y[n-2]$, $y[n-3]$ with the same signs, and the right-hand gains as the coefficients of $x$.

**Parallel example, worked:** "Reading a direct form II diagram" on [[concepts/direct-form-structures|direct form I and II]], and the derivation of eqs. 9–13 above it.

> [!trap] Traps
> - Hertz must become rad/sample before anything is called a band edge, and the answer is asked for $0\le\omega\le\pi$ only.
> - In Problem 2 decide after cancelling, and say which fact you used.
> - Feedback gains: same sign in the LCCDE, opposite sign in the denominator of $H(z)$.
> - In direct form II the feedback gains are on the input side.
