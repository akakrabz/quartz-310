## Problem 1 · True/False (20 pts)

*Topic: DTFT facts ([[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]], [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]]), frequency response of real systems ([[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]]); the DFT, circular convolution and the FFT — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 1
> Mark "True" or "False" for the following statements.
>
> (a) Zero padding a finite-length signal can change its DTFT.
>
> (b) Suppose that $x[n]$ is bandlimited to $\frac{\pi}{4}$. Then, its DTFT $X_d(\omega) = 0$ for $\lvert\omega\rvert \gt \frac{\pi}{4}$.
>
> (c) The DTFT of the finite-length signal $x[n] = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{32}n\right)$, $n = 0, 1, \dots, 15$ is given by the formula $X_d(\omega) = \pi\left[\delta\left(\omega - \frac{\pi}{32}\right) + \delta\left(\omega + \frac{\pi}{32}\right)\right]$.
>
> (d) An LSI system has transfer function $H(z)$. Suppose that the output of the system to the input $x[n] = \cos(\omega_0 n + \phi)$ is $y[n] = \lvert H(e^{j\omega_0})\rvert\cos\left(\omega_0 n + \phi + \angle H(e^{j\omega_0})\right)$ for any $\omega_0, \phi$, where $H(e^{j\omega}) = H(z)\big|_{z=e^{j\omega}}$. Then, the system $h[n]$ can be either real or complex.
>
> (e) The DTFT $X_d(\omega)$ of a discrete-time signal $x[n]$ is periodic only when $x[n]$ has infinite length.
>
> (f) The 64-point DFT of the finite-length signal $x[n] = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{16}n\right)$, $0 \le n \le 63$ has only two nonzero elements.
>
> (g) Linear convolution can be computed as a circular convolution via zero-padding.
>
> (h) Circular convolution can be applied to two finite duration sequences with arbitrary lengths.
>
> (i) Let $\{x[n]\}_{n=0}^{5} = \{1, -1, -2, 3, 4, -3\}$. Consider the corresponding 6-point DFT $\{X[k]\}_{k=0}^{5}$. Then, $X[0] = 0$.
>
> (j) FFT is just an efficient algorithm to evaluate DFT.

> [!success]- Solution 1
> - **(a) False.** Appended zeros add terms $0 \cdot e^{-j\omega n}$ to the DTFT sum, so $X_d(\omega)$ is unchanged. Zero padding changes the **DFT**: it samples the same $X_d$ on a finer grid.
> - **(b) False.** Every DTFT is $2\pi$-periodic, so whatever lies in the band $\lvert\omega\rvert \lt \frac{\pi}{4}$ reappears around $\pm 2\pi$, where $\lvert\omega\rvert \gt \frac{\pi}{4}$: for example $X_d(2\pi) = X_d(0)$, which is not 0 for an ideal lowpass. "Bandlimited to $\frac{\pi}{4}$" means $X_d(\omega) = 0$ for $\frac{\pi}{4} \lt \lvert\omega\rvert \le \pi$.
> - **(c) False.** The impulse pair belongs to the cosine that lasts forever. Sixteen samples give a finite sum: two shifted Dirichlet ("sinc-like") kernels, nonzero almost everywhere.
> - **(d) False.** Split the cosine into exponentials: the output is $\tfrac12\left[H_d(\omega_0)e^{j(\omega_0 n+\phi)} + H_d(-\omega_0)e^{-j(\omega_0 n+\phi)}\right]$. It equals $\lvert H_d(\omega_0)\rvert\cos(\omega_0 n + \phi + \angle H_d(\omega_0))$ for every $\omega_0, \phi$ only if $H_d(-\omega_0) = H_d^*(\omega_0)$: conjugate symmetry, which means $h[n]$ is **real**.
> - **(e) False.** Any DTFT is $2\pi$-periodic, because $e^{-j(\omega+2\pi)n} = e^{-j\omega n}$ for every integer $n$, whatever the length of $x$.
> - **(f) True.** $\frac{\pi}{16} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 2}{64}$: exactly two cycles in 64 samples, so $X[2] = X[62] = 32$ and the other 62 bins are 0.
> - **(g) True.** Pad both sequences to $N \ge L_x + L_h - 1$; the $N$-point circular convolution then equals the linear one (see #11).
> - **(h) False** (the key's answer). An $N$-point circular convolution combines two length-$N$ sequences; sequences of different lengths must first be padded to a common $N$.
> - **(i) False.** $X[0] = \sum_n x[n] = 1 - 1 - 2 + 3 + 4 - 3 = 2$.
> - **(j) True.** The FFT returns exactly the DFT values, in about $N\log_2 N$ operations instead of $N^2$.

> [!note] The key's annotation
> On the posted key, (g), (h) and (j) carry the note "Not responsible for on our Midterm 2!" (circular convolution and the FFT).

> [!trap] Where points go
> - **(b), (e)** test one fact: the DTFT repeats every $2\pi$. "For $\lvert\omega\rvert \gt \frac{\pi}{4}$" includes $\omega = 2\pi$.
> - **(a)** Zero padding never changes the DTFT, only how finely the DFT samples it (compare [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #1(d) and #7(b)).
> - **(c)** Impulses in $X_d(\omega)$ need a sinusoid that never ends; a finite piece of one has a smooth spectrum with sidelobes.

## Problem 2 · DFT bins in hertz (6 pts)

*Topic: spectral analysis with the DFT — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 2
> A real continuous-time signal $x_c(t)$ is bandlimited to frequencies below 20 kHz, i.e., $X_a(\Omega) = 0$ for $\lvert\Omega\rvert \ge 2\pi(20000)$. Suppose that $x_a(t)$ is sampled with sampling frequency $F_s = 40$ kHz to produce $x[n] = x_a(nT_s)$ and 1000 samples are extracted corresponding to $n = 0, 1, \dots, 999$. Let $\{X[k]\}_{k=0}^{999}$ be the 1000-point DFT of $\{x[n]\}_{n=0}^{999}$.
>
> (a) To what continuous-time frequencies do the indices $k = 300$ and $k = 800$ correspond? Provide your answers in Hz.
>
> (b) What is the spacing between the DFT samples in Hz (continuous-time frequency spacing)?

> [!success]- Solution 2
> Bin $k$ sits at $\omega_k = \frac{2\pi k}{N}$, and $\omega = \Omega T$ converts it to analog frequency. Bins past $N/2$ are negative frequencies, because $\omega_k$ and $\omega_k - 2\pi$ are the same digital frequency.
>
> **(a)** $\omega_{300} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 300}{1000} = \frac{3\pi}{5}$, so $\Omega_{300} = \omega_{300}/T = \frac{3\pi}{5}\cdot 40000 = 24000\pi$ rad/s:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{F_{300} = 12\ \text{kHz}}
> $$
> $\omega_{800} = \frac{8\pi}{5} \equiv \frac{8\pi}{5} - 2\pi = -\frac{2\pi}{5}$, so $\Omega_{800} = -16000\pi$ rad/s:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{F_{800} = -8\ \text{kHz}}
> $$
> (for the real $x_c$, the mirror image of the +8 kHz component at $k = 200$).
>
> **(b)** $\Delta\omega = \frac{2\pi}{N} = \frac{\pi}{500}$, $\Delta\Omega = \Delta\omega/T = 80\pi$ rad/s:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{\Delta F = \frac{F_s}{N} = 40\ \text{Hz}}
> $$
> (checked: cosines at 12 kHz and 8 kHz sampled at 40 kHz put their peaks at $k = 300$ and $k = 800$.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - "No aliasing!" is the key's margin note: the band ends at $F_s/2$, so every bin maps to exactly one analog frequency in $(-20, 20)$ kHz. Answering 32 kHz for $k = 800$ forgets that the upper half of the DFT holds negative frequencies.
> - The spacing is $F_s/N$ in hertz; $\frac{2\pi}{N}$ is the spacing in rad/sample.

## Problem 3 · DTFT values without the DTFT (9 pts)

*Topic: computing DTFTs — [[problems/dtft-and-inverse-dtft|DTFT and inverse DTFT]] · [[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]].*

> [!question] Problem 3
> Consider the following signal:
> $$
> x[n] = \delta[n+3] - 3\delta[n+2] + 5\delta[n+1] - 7\delta[n] + 5\delta[n-1] - 3\delta[n-2] + \delta[n-3]
> $$
> Compute the following quantities:
>
> (a) $X_d(0)$
>
> (b) $X_d(\pi)$
>
> (c) $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X_d(\omega)\,d\omega$

> [!success]- Solution 3
> $x = \{1,\ -3,\ 5,\ \underset{\uparrow}{-7},\ 5,\ -3,\ 1\}$ and $X_d(\omega) = \sum_n x[n]e^{-j\omega n}$.
>
> **(a)** $e^{-j0n} = 1$: $X_d(0) = \sum_n x[n] = 1 - 3 + 5 - 7 + 5 - 3 + 1 = \boldsymbol{-1}$.
>
> **(b)** $e^{-j\pi n} = (-1)^n$: $X_d(\pi) = \sum_n x[n]\,(-1)^n = -1 - 3 - 5 - 7 - 5 - 3 - 1 = \boldsymbol{-25}$. Every term is negative because the signs of $x$ alternate in step with $(-1)^n$.
>
> **(c)** This is the inverse DTFT at $n = 0$: $x[0] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X_d(\omega)\,d\omega$, so
> $$
> \boldsymbol{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X_d(\omega)\,d\omega = 2\pi x[0] = -14\pi}
> $$
> Cross-check: $x$ is real and even, so $X_d(\omega) = -7 + 10\cos\omega - 6\cos 2\omega + 2\cos 3\omega$, which gives $-1$ at $\omega = 0$ and $-25$ at $\omega = \pi$; integrating it over one period keeps only $2\pi \cdot (-7)$. (checked: a trapezoid integral of the DTFT on a dense grid gives $-43.98230 = -14\pi$.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - **(c)** needs no integration: it is $2\pi$ times a sample. Dropping the $2\pi$ (answering $-7$) is the usual loss.
> - **(b)** $e^{-j\pi n} = (-1)^n$ also for negative $n$: $(-1)^{-3} = -1$.

## Problem 4 · A linear phase on the DFT (5 pts)

*Topic: the DFT and its properties (circular shift) — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 4
> Let $\{X[k]\}_{k=0}^{7}$ be the 8-point DFT of $x[n] = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$. Determine the sequence $\{y[n]\}_{n=0}^{7}$ whose DFT is $Y[k] = e^{-j\frac{\pi}{4}k}X[k]$, $k = 0, 1, \dots, 7$.

> [!success]- Solution 4
> Circular shift property: $x[\langle n - m\rangle_N] \leftrightarrow e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}km}X[k]$. Here $e^{-j\frac{\pi}{4}k} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{8}k\cdot 1}$, so $N = 8$, $m = 1$:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{y[n] = x[\langle n-1\rangle_8] = \{\underset{\uparrow}{8},\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7\}}
> $$
> The last sample wraps around to $n = 0$.

```python
import numpy as np
x = np.arange(1, 9)                       # x[n] = 1, 2, ..., 8
k = np.arange(8)
y = np.fft.ifft(np.exp(-1j*np.pi/4*k) * np.fft.fft(x))
print(np.round(y.real, 12))
```

```text
[8. 1. 2. 3. 4. 5. 6. 7.]
```

> [!trap] Where points go
> - The shift is **circular**: a linear delay would give a length-9 sequence $\{0, 1, \dots, 8\}$, which is not an 8-point answer.
> - $e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}km}$ with $m \gt 0$ is a delay (shift right), the same sign rule as the DTFT's $e^{-j\omega m}$.

## Problem 5 · Inverse DTFT of a linear phase (8 pts)

*Topic: inverse DTFT — [[problems/dtft-and-inverse-dtft|DTFT and inverse DTFT]] · [[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]], [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]].*

> [!question] Problem 5
> Give a closed-form expression for $x[n]$, valid for all $n$, given that its DTFT is $X_d(\omega) = e^{-j\frac{\omega}{3}}$ for $\lvert\omega\rvert \le \pi$. Your answer should not include complex numbers.

> [!success]- Solution 5
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> x[n] &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-j\frac{\omega}{3}}e^{j\omega n}\,d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{e^{j\omega(n-\frac13)}}{j(n-\frac13)}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi}\\[4pt]
> &= \frac{1}{\pi(n-\frac13)}\cdot\frac{e^{j\pi(n-\frac13)} - e^{-j\pi(n-\frac13)}}{2j}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $$
> \boldsymbol{x[n] = \frac{\sin\left(\pi\left(n - \frac13\right)\right)}{\pi\left(n - \frac13\right)}}
> $$
> No division by zero: $n - \frac13 \ne 0$ for integer $n$. Since $\sin(\pi n - \frac{\pi}{3}) = -(-1)^n\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$, the same sequence is $x[n] = (-1)^{n+1}\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi(3n-1)}$; for example $x[0] = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi} \approx 0.83$. (checked: a numerical inverse DTFT matches both forms for $n = -4, \dots, 4$.)

> [!intuition] A delay of a third of a sample
> $e^{-j\omega n_0}$ is a delay by $n_0$ samples. With $n_0 = \frac13$ there is no sample to move to, so the "delayed impulse" becomes the band-limited sinc $\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi t}$ sampled a third of a sample off its peak: a fractional-delay filter.

> [!trap] Where points go
> - Answering $\delta[n - \frac13]$: the delay property only works for integer shifts.
> - The key writes the answer as "$\mathrm{Sinc}(\pi(n - \frac13))$" with $\mathrm{Sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin u}{u}$. NumPy's `np.sinc` is the normalized one: `np.sinc(n - 1/3)`.

## Problem 6 · Can the output be real? (5 pts)

*Topic: DTFT symmetry (real sequence ⇔ conjugate-symmetric DTFT) — [[problems/dtft-and-inverse-dtft|DTFT and inverse DTFT]] · [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]].*

> [!question] Problem 6
> A sequence $x[n]$ with DTFT $X_d(\omega) = e^{-j\frac{\omega^2}{3}}$ is input to an LSI system with unit-pulse response $h[n] = \left(\frac13\right)^n u[n]$. Can the output sequence $y[n]$ be real? Explain.

> [!success]- Solution 6
> **No.** A sequence is real exactly when its DTFT is conjugate symmetric, $Y_d(-\omega) = Y_d^*(\omega)$.
>
> - $X_d(-\omega) = e^{-j\frac{\omega^2}{3}}$ but $X_d^*(\omega) = e^{+j\frac{\omega^2}{3}}$: they differ wherever $\sin\frac{\omega^2}{3} \ne 0$, so $x[n]$ is complex.
> - $h$ is real, so $H_d(-\omega) = H_d^*(\omega)$ with $H_d(\omega) = \dfrac{1}{1 - \frac13 e^{-j\omega}}$. Then
> $$
> Y_d(-\omega) - Y_d^*(\omega) = \left[X_d(-\omega) - X_d^*(\omega)\right]H_d^*(\omega).
> $$
> - $H_d$ never vanishes: $\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert \ge \frac{1}{1 + 1/3} = \frac34$. So the bracket survives, $Y_d$ is not conjugate symmetric, and $\boldsymbol{y[n]}$ **is complex**.
>
> (checked: numerical inverse DTFTs of $X_d$ and $X_d H_d$ both have nonzero imaginary parts.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - The key stops at "$x[n]$ is complex, therefore $y[n] = x[n]*h[n]$ cannot be real-valued." That step needs the third bullet: a filter that is zero where $x$'s non-symmetric part lives *could* return a real output. Here $\lvert H_d\rvert \ge \frac34$, so the conclusion holds.
> - $X_d$ is **even** in $\omega$ ($\omega^2$), which makes $x[n]$ even, $x[-n] = x[n]$, but not real.

## Problem 7 · Sinusoids through $1 + z^{-4}$ (10 pts)

*Topic: LTI response to complex exponentials and sinusoids — [[problems/lti-response-to-sinusoids|LTI response to sinusoids]] · [[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]].*

> [!question] Problem 7
> Consider an LSI system characterized by the transfer function $H(z) = 1 + z^{-4}$. Compute the system response, $y[n]$, to the input
> $$
> x[n] = 3 + 4\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}n\right) + e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}.
> $$

> [!success]- Solution 7
> FIR, so the ROC contains the unit circle and $H_d(\omega) = H(e^{j\omega})$:
> $$
> H_d(\omega) = 1 + e^{-j4\omega} = e^{-j2\omega}\left(e^{j2\omega} + e^{-j2\omega}\right) = 2\cos(2\omega)\,e^{-j2\omega}.
> $$
> - DC: $H_d(0) = 2$, so $3 \mapsto 6$.
> - $\omega = \frac{\pi}{4}$: $H_d(\frac{\pi}{4}) = 2\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\,e^{-j\pi/2} = 0$. The cosine is removed (both of its exponentials, since $H_d(-\frac{\pi}{4}) = 0$ too).
> - $\omega = \frac{\pi}{2}$: $H_d(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 2\cos(\pi)\,e^{-j\pi} = (-2)(-1) = 2$, so $e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n} \mapsto 2e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}$.
> $$
> \boldsymbol{y[n] = 6 + 2e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}}
> $$
> Time-domain check: $y[n] = x[n] + x[n-4]$; $\cos(\frac{\pi}{4}(n-4)) = -\cos(\frac{\pi}{4}n)$ cancels and $e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}(n-4)} = e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}$ doubles. (checked numerically for $-10 \le n \lt 30$.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - $2\cos(2\omega)$ is an amplitude, not a magnitude: at $\omega = \frac{\pi}{2}$ it is $-2$, and the sign combines with $e^{-j\pi} = -1$ to give $+2$. Magnitude: $\lvert H_d\rvert = 2\lvert\cos 2\omega\rvert$.
> - Treat the complex exponential on its own: it has no partner at $-\frac{\pi}{2}$, so the output keeps the form $2e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}$ (not a cosine).

## Problem 8 · D/A conversion of $\delta[n-3]$ (10 pts)

*Topic: sampling and reconstruction (D/A converters, zero-order hold) — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 8
> The discrete-time signal $x[n] = \delta[n-3]$ goes through a digital-to-analog (D/A) converter with interpolating interval $T$. Determine and sketch $x_a(t)$ for the following cases (label your graphs carefully):
>
> (a) The D/A is a ZOH
>
> (b) The D/A is an ideal D/A

> [!note] The key's annotation
> Part (a) is crossed out on the posted key: "ZOH is not on our Midterm 2!" It is solved here anyway.

> [!success]- Solution 8
> A D/A builds $x_a(t) = \sum_n x[n]\,p(t - nT)$ from one pulse shape $p$ per sample. With a single sample $x[3] = 1$, the output is one pulse at $t = 3T$.
>
> **(a) ZOH:** $p(t) = 1$ for $0 \le t \lt T$ (hold each sample for one interval):
> $$
> \boldsymbol{x_a(t) = \begin{cases}1, & 3T \le t \lt 4T\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}}
> $$
> **(b) Ideal D/A:** $p(t) = \dfrac{\sin(\pi t/T)}{\pi t/T}$ (ideal lowpass interpolation):
> $$
> \boldsymbol{x_a(t) = \frac{\sin\left(\pi(t - 3T)/T\right)}{\pi(t-3T)/T}}
> $$
> Peak 1 at $t = 3T$, zero at every other multiple of $T$.

> [!success]- Solution 9(a)–(b)
> **(a)** $\Omega_{\max} = 1000\pi$ rad/s is $F_{\max} = 500$ Hz, so the Nyquist rate is 1 kHz and
> $$
> \boldsymbol{T_{NYQ} = \frac{\pi}{\Omega_{\max}} = 10^{-3}\ \text{s}}
> $$
> **(b) Yes.** With $T \lt T_{NYQ}$ the spectral copies do not overlap, the A/D's gain $\frac1T$ and the D/A's gain $T$ cancel, and the chain acts on $X_a(\Omega)$ through $H_d(\Omega T)$:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{H_a(\Omega) = H_d(\Omega T) = \begin{cases}1, & \lvert\Omega\rvert \lt \dfrac{\pi}{3T}\\[4pt] 0, & \dfrac{\pi}{3T} \lt \lvert\Omega\rvert \lt 1000\pi\end{cases}}
> $$
> an ideal lowpass with cutoff $\Omega_c = \frac{\pi}{3T}$ rad/s (the key's plot). If $T \lt \frac{1}{3000}$ s, the cutoff lies beyond $1000\pi$ and $H_a = 1$ on the whole band.

> [!success]- Solution 9(c)
> **Yes.** Above the Nyquist interval the copies overlap, but $H_d$ only keeps $\lvert\omega\rvert \lt \frac{\pi}{3}$. The baseband copy covers $\lvert\omega\rvert \le 1000\pi T$; the copy centred at $2\pi$ starts at $2\pi - 1000\pi T$. Keep that edge out of the passband:
> $$
> 2\pi - 1000\pi T \ge \frac{\pi}{3} \iff 1000\pi T \le \frac{5\pi}{3} \iff \boldsymbol{T \le \frac{5}{3000} = \frac{1}{600}\ \text{s}}

> [!note] The key's annotation
> "FFT not on our Midterm 2!" The key gives no answer. The solution below uses the butterfly of the Singer–Munson course notes, whose own worked example (part of a 64-point DIT FFT) is the same kind of question.

> [!success]- Solution 10
> In a radix-2 DIT flow graph (bit-reversed input, natural-order output) the last stage combines the 16-point DFTs $Y_p$ (even-indexed samples) and $Z_p$ (odd-indexed samples):
> $$
> X_p = Y_p + W_{32}^{p}Z_p,\qquad X_{p+16} = Y_p - W_{32}^{p}Z_p,\qquad 0 \le p \le 15,\quad W_N = e^{-j2\pi/N}.
> $$
> In the notes' butterfly the top node feeds both outputs with weight 1; the bottom node feeds $X_p$ with $W_N^p$ and $X_{p+N/2}$ with $-W_N^p$.
>
> - **$\alpha$:** $X_\alpha$ and $X_{27}$ leave the same last-stage butterfly, so they differ by 16: $\boldsymbol{\alpha = 11}$.
> - **$a$:** top node $Y_{11}$ to top output $X_{11}$: $\boldsymbol{a = 1}$.
> - **$c$:** bottom node $Z_{11}$ to bottom output $X_{27}$: $\boldsymbol{c = -W_{32}^{11} = -e^{-j11\pi/16}}$.
> - **$b$:** the other butterfly pairs rows 3 and 19, and $b$ runs from $Z_3$ to $X_{19}$: $\boldsymbol{b = -W_{32}^{3} = -e^{-j3\pi/16}}$.
> - **$d$:** stage 4 builds $Z$ from the 8-point DFTs $Y'_p$, $Z'_p$ of $x[4m+1]$ and $x[4m+3]$: $Z_p = Y'_p + W_{16}^p Z'_p$, $Z_{p+8} = Y'_p - W_{16}^p Z'_p$. Rows 19 and 27 are $p = 3$, and $d$ runs bottom to bottom: $\boldsymbol{d = -W_{16}^{3} = -W_{32}^{6} = -e^{-j3\pi/8}}$.
> - **$\beta$:** row 27 is fed by the input at the bit-reversed index: $27 = 11011_2$ reads $11011_2$ backwards, so $\boldsymbol{\beta = 27}$.
>
> (checked: a 32-point DIT FFT assembled from these butterflies reproduces `np.fft.fft`, and its branch weights at those four positions are $1$, $-W_{32}^3$, $-W_{32}^{11}$, $-W_{16}^3$.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - Conventions differ between books (some put $W_N^p$ on the bottom input and $\pm1$ on the outputs). Use the notes' butterfly and say so.
> - $\beta$ needs bit reversal; 27 happens to be a palindrome in 5 bits, so either ordering gives 27.

## Problem 11 · Zero padding for fast convolution (8 pts)

*Topic: linear vs circular convolution, the FFT — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 11
> You are given two finite-duration sequences: $\{x_n\}_{n=0}^{14}$ and $\{h_n\}_{n=0}^{7}$, and you wish to compute their linear convolution using (a) a DFT approach with appropriate zero-padding (namely, $\{x_n\}_{n=0}^{14} * \{h_n\}_{n=0}^{7} = \mathrm{DFT}^{-1}\{\mathrm{DFT}\{x_n\}\cdot\mathrm{DFT}\{h_n\}\}$) and (b) a radix-2, DIT FFT algorithm. What is the minimum number of zeros that must be padded to each of the sequences in order to compute $(x*h)$ using each approach?

> [!note] The key's annotation
> "FFT + circular convolution not on our Midterm 2!" The key gives no answer.

> [!success]- Solution 11
> $x$ has 15 samples and $h$ has 8, so $x*h$ has $15 + 8 - 1 = 22$. Circular convolution equals linear convolution once both are padded to $N \ge 22$.
>
> | approach | $N$ | zeros added to $x$ | zeros added to $h$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | DFT | 22 | **7** | **14** |
> | radix-2 FFT | 32 (next power of 2) | **17** | **24** |
>
> (checked: 22-point circular convolution of random sequences of lengths 15 and 8 equals `np.convolve`; 21 points do not.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - $N$ must reach $L_x + L_h - 1$, not $\max(L_x, L_h)$; [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2021|FA2021 MT2]] #6(c) asks the same thing.
> - Both sequences are padded to the **same** $N$, so they get different numbers of zeros.
