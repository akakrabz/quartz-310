## Problem 1 · True/False (16 pts)

*Topic: DTFT existence, inverse and symmetry ([[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]], [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]]); sampling, ideal D/A and the DFT: Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 1
> Select **True** or **False** for each statement.
>
> (a) A discrete-time signal $x[n]$ with z-transform $X(z)$ always has a finite DTFT $X_d(\omega)$ given by $X(z)$ evaluated at $z = e^{j\omega}$, i.e. evaluate $X(z)$ along the unit circle.
>
> (b) Let $x[n]$ be a discrete-time signal with DTFT $X_d(\omega) = e^{-j\omega/2}$, $-\pi \le \omega \le \pi$. The corresponding signal is $x[n] = \delta\big[n - \tfrac12\big]$.
>
> (c) The DTFT of a real-valued signal is always Hermitian symmetric.
>
> (d) Every continuous-time signal has a minimum sampling frequency to avoid aliasing during analog-to-digital conversion.
>
> (e) A discrete-time signal is given by $x[n] = 4\delta[n-2]$. We pass $x[n]$ to an ideal D/A converter with sampling period $T$ to recover $x_a(t)$. The recovered continuous-time signal is $x_a(t) = 4\delta(n - 2T)$.
>
> (f) Let $\{x[n]\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$ be a length-$N$ signal with DTFT $X_d(\omega)$ and $\{y[n]\}_{n=0}^{M+N-1}$ be $x[n]$ zero-padded with $M$ zeros. The DTFT of $y[n]$, $Y_d(\omega)$, is equal to $X_d(\omega)$.
>
> (g) The DFT of $\{x[n]\}_{n=0}^{23} = \cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{8}n\right)$ will only have two non-zero values.
>
> (h) The DTFT and DFT of a length-14 sequence $x[n]$ are given by $X_d(\omega)$ and $X[k]$, respectively. We know then that $X[11] = X_d\left(-\tfrac{3\pi}{7}\right)$.

> [!success]- Solution 1
> - **(a) False.** $X_d(\omega) = X(z)\big|_{z=e^{j\omega}}$ only when the ROC contains the unit circle. $x[n] = 2^nu[n]$ has $X(z) = \frac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}$, $\lvert z\rvert \gt 2$, but no DTFT: $\sum\lvert x[n]\rvert = \infty$.
> - **(b) False.** $n$ is an integer, so "$\delta[n - \frac12]$" is not a signal. The inverse DTFT gives a sinc sampled half-way between its zeros: $x[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{j\omega(n-\frac12)}d\omega = \dfrac{\sin\big(\pi(n-\frac12)\big)}{\pi(n-\frac12)}$, with $x[0] = x[1] = \frac{2}{\pi}$ (checked: numerical inverse DTFT).
> - **(c) True.** For real $x[n]$, $X_d(-\omega) = X_d^*(\omega)$: even magnitude, odd phase ([[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]]).
> - **(d) False.** Only a **band-limited** signal has a Nyquist rate. A signal with energy at arbitrarily high frequencies (a rectangular pulse, for instance) aliases at every sampling rate.
> - **(e) False.** An ideal D/A interpolates with sincs, so one sample becomes one sinc: $x_a(t) = 4\,\dfrac{\sin\big(\pi(t-2T)/T\big)}{\pi(t-2T)/T}$. (The statement also mixes $n$ and $t$.)
> - **(f) True.** The appended zeros add nothing to $\sum_n x[n]e^{-j\omega n}$. Zero-padding changes the **DFT** (more samples of the same DTFT), never the DTFT.
> - **(g) False.** $\cos(\frac{\pi}{8}n)$ has period 16, so 24 samples hold 1.5 periods: $\frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{2\pi k}{24}$ needs $k = 1.5$. The energy leaks into every bin; all 24 values are nonzero (checked with `np.fft.fft`).
> - **(h) True.** $X[k] = X_d\big(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\big)$, so $X[11] = X_d\big(\frac{22\pi}{14}\big) = X_d\big(\frac{11\pi}{7}\big) = X_d\big(\frac{11\pi}{7} - 2\pi\big) = X_d\big(-\frac{3\pi}{7}\big)$ by $2\pi$-periodicity.

> [!trap] Where points go
> - **(a)** "Has a z-transform" is not "has a DTFT": check that the ROC contains $\lvert z\rvert = 1$. [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2]] #1(a) asks the same with $2^nu[n]$.
> - **(f) vs (g)** Zero-padding never changes $X_d$; it only changes which frequencies the DFT samples. A cosine gives exactly two nonzero DFT values only when the record holds a **whole number of periods**, as in [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #9.

## Problem 2 · $X_d(0)$ and $X_d(\pi)$ without the transform (6 pts)

*Topic: computing a DTFT — [[3-fourier-analysis/13-fourier-analysis-and-the-dtft|Lecture 13]], [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]] · [[problems/dtft-and-inverse-dtft|DTFTs and inverse DTFTs]].*

> [!question] Problem 2
> Suppose that $x[n] = u[n+2] - u[n-3]$, where $u[n]$ is the step sequence. Calculate $X_d(0)$ and $X_d(\pi)$.

> [!success]- Solution 2
> $x[n] = \{1,\ 1,\ \underset{\uparrow}{1},\ 1,\ 1\}$ (ones for $-2 \le n \le 2$). With $e^{-j0n} = 1$ and $e^{-j\pi n} = (-1)^n$:
> $$
> X_d(0) = \sum_n x[n] = \boldsymbol{5},\qquad X_d(\pi) = \sum_n (-1)^nx[n] = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 = \boldsymbol{1}.
> $$
> (The full transform is $X_d(\omega) = \dfrac{\sin(5\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}$: it tends to 5 at $\omega = 0$ and equals $\frac{\sin(5\pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} = 1$ at $\pi$.)

> [!trap] Where points go
> - There is no need to find $X_d(\omega)$: $\omega = 0$ sums the samples, $\omega = \pi$ alternates their signs. The signs are anchored at $n = 0$ (the arrow), so the end samples at $n = \pm2$ count $+1$.

## Problem 3 · Sampling a sine: which $\Omega_0$? (6 pts)

*Topic: sampling and aliasing — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes).*

> [!question] Problem 3
> Suppose we sample $x_a(t) = \sin(\Omega_0t)$ at sampling frequency $f_s = 400$ Hz to obtain $x[n] = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}n\right)$. Which of the following are possible values of $\Omega_0$? (Circle all that apply.)
>
> (a) $-600\pi$ · (b) $-400\pi$ · (c) $200\pi$ · (d) $400\pi$ · (e) $800\pi$ · (f) $1200\pi$

> [!success]- Solution 3
> Sampling gives $x[n] = \sin(\Omega_0nT) = \sin\left(\frac{\Omega_0}{400}n\right)$. A $2\pi$ shift of the digital frequency is invisible, so we need $\frac{\Omega_0}{400} = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k$:
> $$
> \Omega_0 = 200\pi + 800\pi k = \dots,\ -600\pi,\ 200\pi,\ 1000\pi,\ \dots\quad\Rightarrow\quad\textbf{(a) and (c)}
> $$
> The other four give $\frac{\Omega_0}{400} \in \{-\pi, \pi, 2\pi, 3\pi\}$: samples $\sin(m\pi n) = 0$ for every $n$ (checked sample by sample).

> [!trap] Where points go
> - **(a)** A negative $\Omega_0$ can work: $-600\pi/400 = -\frac{3\pi}{2} \equiv \frac{\pi}{2}$. But a sine is odd, so $\Omega_0 = -200\pi$ would give $-\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}n)$: for a sine the sign of the folded frequency matters, for a cosine it would not.
> - Frequencies at multiples of $\frac{f_s}{2}$ ($\pm400\pi$, $800\pi$, $1200\pi$ rad/s here) put every sample on a zero of the sine.

## Problem 4 · A frequency response with an even phase (18 pts)

*Topic: magnitude and phase response, LTI response to exponentials and sinusoids — [[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]], [[3-fourier-analysis/16-magnitude-and-phase-response|Lecture 16]] · [[problems/magnitude-phase-and-group-delay|magnitude, phase and group delay]] · [[problems/lti-response-to-sinusoids|LTI response to sinusoids]].*

> [!question] Problem 4
> Consider an LTI system with frequency response
> $$
> H_d(\omega) = \left(\frac12 + \cos\omega\right)e^{j\lvert\omega\rvert},\qquad -\pi \le \omega \le \pi .
> $$
> (a) Plot the magnitude response $\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert$ and phase response $\angle H_d(\omega)$. Label your axes carefully.
>
> (b) Compute the output of this system to the input signal $x[n] = 4j - 2e^{j\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}n + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)} + 3\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right)$.

> [!success]- Solution 4(a)
> The factor $A(\omega) = \frac12 + \cos\omega$ is real but changes sign where $\cos\omega = -\frac12$, at $\omega = \pm\frac{2\pi}{3}$. A magnitude is never negative, and a negative $A$ contributes a phase of $\pm\pi$ (take $-\pi$ to stay inside $[-\pi, \pi]$):
> $$
> \lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert = \left\lvert\tfrac12 + \cos\omega\right\rvert,\qquad
> \angle H_d(\omega) = \begin{cases}\lvert\omega\rvert, & \lvert\omega\rvert \lt \frac{2\pi}{3}\\[2pt] \lvert\omega\rvert - \pi, & \frac{2\pi}{3} \lt \lvert\omega\rvert \le \pi .\end{cases}
> $$
> Key values: $\lvert H_d\rvert = \frac32$ at 0, 0 at $\pm\frac{2\pi}{3}$, $\frac12$ at $\pm\pi$. The phase rises from 0 to $\frac{2\pi}{3}$, drops by $\pi$ to $-\frac{\pi}{3}$ at $\pm\frac{2\pi}{3}$, and climbs back to 0 at $\pm\pi$.

> [!success]- Solution 4(b)
> **Is $h[n]$ real? No.** A real $h[n]$ has an odd phase; here the phase is even (the factor $e^{j\lvert\omega\rvert}$), so $h[n]$ is complex and the shortcut $\sin \to \lvert H_d\rvert\sin(\cdot + \angle H_d)$ is **not** allowed. Write every term as complex exponentials and use $e^{j\omega_0n} \to H_d(\omega_0)e^{j\omega_0n}$ ([[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]]):
> $$
> H_d(0) = \tfrac32,\qquad H_d\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = 0,\qquad H_d\left(\pm\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right) = \left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)e^{j3\pi/4} = \tfrac{\sqrt2 - 1}{2}\,e^{-j\pi/4}.
> $$
> - The constant $4j = 4j\,e^{j0n}$ becomes $4j\cdot\frac32 = 6j$.
> - The exponential at $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ sits on a zero of $H_d$ and disappears.
> - $3\sin(\frac{3\pi}{4}n) = \frac{3}{2j}\big(e^{j\frac{3\pi}{4}n} - e^{-j\frac{3\pi}{4}n}\big)$, and both exponentials see the **same** factor $\frac{\sqrt2-1}{2}e^{-j\pi/4}$ because $H_d$ is even.
> $$
> \boldsymbol{y[n] = 6j + \frac{3(\sqrt2-1)}{4j}\,e^{j\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)} - \frac{3(\sqrt2-1)}{4j}\,e^{-j\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)}}
> $$
> The key also gives $y[n] = 6j + \frac{3(\sqrt2-1)}{4}e^{j(\frac{3\pi}{4}n - \frac{3\pi}{4})} - \frac{3(\sqrt2-1)}{4}e^{-j(\frac{3\pi}{4}n + \frac{3\pi}{4})}$ (absorbing $\frac1j = e^{-j\pi/2}$). Since both exponentials got the same factor, the most compact form is
> $$
> y[n] = 6j + \frac{3(\sqrt2-1)}{2}\,e^{-j\pi/4}\,\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}n\right).
> $$
> (checked: the three forms agree, and convolving the input with $h[n]$ from a numerical inverse DTFT reproduces them)

> - **(b)** Using the real-sinusoid rule $3\lvert H_d\rvert\sin\big(\frac{3\pi}{4}n + \angle H_d\big)$: it needs a Hermitian $H_d$ (real $h$), which this one is not, and it gives a different, real answer. > [!success]- Solution 5
> Sampling maps $\Omega$ to $\omega = \Omega T$ and repeats the spectrum every $2\pi$ with height $\frac1T$: $X_d(\omega) = \frac1T\sum_k X_c\big(\frac{\omega - 2\pi k}{T}\big)$. A frequency of $f$ hertz lands at $\omega = 2\pi fT$.
>
> **(a)** Only the band $\lvert f\rvert \le 5$ kHz has to survive un-aliased: whatever lands on top of 5–10 kHz is deleted by $H_d$ anyway. The passband edge is at $2\pi\cdot5000\,T$, and the copy centred at $2\pi$ starts at $2\pi - 2\pi\cdot10^4\,T$. No overlap inside the passband:
> $$
> 2\pi\cdot5000\,T \le 2\pi - 2\pi\cdot10^4\,T \iff T \le \frac{1}{15000}\ \text{s} \approx 66.67\ \mu\text{s},
> $$
> so the largest period is $\boldsymbol{T = \frac{1}{15000}}$ s (15 kHz, below the 20 kHz Nyquist rate), and the cutoff is $\omega_c = 2\pi\cdot5000\cdot\frac{1}{15000} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{H_d(\omega) = \begin{cases}1, & \lvert\omega\rvert \le \frac{2\pi}{3}\\ 0, & \frac{2\pi}{3} \lt \lvert\omega\rvert \le \pi\end{cases}}
> $$
> **(b)** Now the band next to $\omega = \pi$ must be kept, and that is where aliasing starts (copies creep in from $\pm\pi$ toward 0). The D/C can also only rebuild frequencies below $\frac{\pi}{T}$ rad/s, which has to reach 10 kHz. Either way: no aliasing at all, sample at the Nyquist rate, $\boldsymbol{T = \frac{1}{20000}}$ s $= 50\ \mu$s. Then 5 kHz lands at $\omega = 2\pi\cdot5000\cdot\frac{1}{20000} = \frac{\pi}{2}$:
> $$
> \boldsymbol{H_d(\omega) = \begin{cases}0, & \lvert\omega\rvert \le \frac{\pi}{2}\\ 1, & \frac{\pi}{2} \lt \lvert\omega\rvert \le \pi\end{cases}}
> $$

> $$

> $$
> \boldsymbol{\frac{7\pi}{16} = \frac{14\pi}{32} \le \lambda_0 \le \frac{15\pi}{32}}
> $$

>

## Problem 7 · The DFT conjugation property (18 pts)

*Topic: DFT properties — Lectures 17+ (not yet in these notes). The DTFT version, $x^*[n] \leftrightarrow X_d^*(-\omega)$, is in [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]].*

> [!question] Problem 7
> The three parts of this problem are related.
>
> (a) Let $\{b[n]\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$ be a length-$N$ **complex-valued** sequence and let $\{B[k]\}_{k=0}^{N-1}$ be the corresponding DFT of $b[n]$. The conjugation property of the DFT states
> $$
> \{b^*[n]\}_{n=0}^{N-1} \overset{\text{DFT}}{\longleftrightarrow} \{B^*[N-k]\}_{k=0}^{N-1}.
> $$
> Prove that the conjugation property holds. *Hint:* use the definition of the DFT, apply conjugation, and replace the integer $k$.
>
> (b) Specify the value $B^*[N]$ in terms of the original DFT $\{B[k]\}_{k=0}^{N-1}$.
>
> (c) We now use the conjugation property to compute the DFTs of two real-valued signals from a single DFT of a complex-valued signal. Let $\{x[n]\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$ and $\{y[n]\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$ be two real-valued sequences with DFTs $X[k]$ and $Y[k]$. Let $z[n] = x[n] + jy[n]$ with DFT $Z[k]$. Given that
> $$
> x[n] = \tfrac12\big(z[n] + z^*[n]\big),\qquad y[n] = \tfrac{1}{2j}\big(z[n] - z^*[n]\big),
> $$
> use the linearity of the DFT and the property in part (a) to write $X[k]$ and $Y[k]$ in terms of the single DFT $Z[k]$.

> [!success]- Solution 7
> **(a)** Start from the definition and pull the conjugate outside (the conjugate of $e^{-j\theta}$ is $e^{j\theta}$), then multiply by $e^{-j\frac{2\pi N}{N}n} = e^{-j2\pi n} = 1$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathrm{DFT}\{b^*[n]\}[k] &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b^*[n]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi k}{N}n} = \Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b[n]\,e^{j\frac{2\pi k}{N}n}\Big)^*\\
> &= \Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b[n]\,e^{j\frac{2\pi k}{N}n}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi N}{N}n}\Big)^* = \Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b[n]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi(N-k)}{N}n}\Big)^* = B^*[N-k].
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **(b)** The DFT formula is $N$-periodic in $k$, so $\boldsymbol{B^*[N] = B^*[0]}$: the $k = 0$ entry of the property reads $N - k$ modulo $N$.
>
> **(c)** By linearity and (a), $\mathrm{DFT}\{z^*[n]\} = Z^*[N-k]$, so
> $$
> \boldsymbol{X[k] = \tfrac12\big(Z[k] + Z^*[N-k]\big),\qquad Y[k] = \tfrac{1}{2j}\big(Z[k] - Z^*[N-k]\big)}
> $$
> with $Z^*[N]$ read as $Z^*[0]$. One complex $N$-point DFT delivers the DFTs of two real signals (checked: random real $x$, $y$ with $N = 12$ against `np.fft.fft`).

> [!trap] Where points go
> - **(a)** Conjugating $b[n]$ but not the exponential, or "proving" the property for one particular $b[n]$.
> - **(c)** $Y[k]$ carries $\frac{1}{2j}$, not $\frac12$. And $X[k]$ is **not** $\mathrm{Re}\{Z[k]\}$: $X[k]$ is itself complex, which is why the index $N - k$ appears.
