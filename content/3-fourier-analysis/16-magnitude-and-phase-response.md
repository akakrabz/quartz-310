*Lecture 16 · Mon Oct 5, 2026 (continued Wed Oct 7) · notes + slides "Magnitude and phase response" · prev: [[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]] · next: Lecture 17 (coming)*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Write the frequency response in polar form, $H_d(\omega)=|H_d(\omega)|\,e^{j\angle H_d(\omega)}$, and plot the two parts separately over $[-\pi,\pi]$. The **magnitude response** $|H_d(\omega)|\ge 0$ is the gain at each frequency (on a linear scale, or as $20\log_{10}|H_d|$ in dB); the **phase response** $\angle H_d(\omega)$, kept as a principal angle in $[-\pi,\pi]$, is the shift. For short FIR filters one trick does all the work: pull out $e^{-jM\omega}$, with $M$ the centre of $h$, so that what is left is a **real** function $A(\omega)$. Then $|H_d|=|A|$, and $\angle H_d$ is the line $-M\omega$ (plus a constant), plus $\pm\pi$ wherever $A(\omega)\lt 0$, because $-1=e^{\pm j\pi}$. So a phase plot has two kinds of breaks: **$\pm\pi$ jumps** where the real amplitude changes sign (a zero of $H$ on the unit circle), and **$2\pi$ wraps** that only keep the angle inside $[-\pi,\pi]$. A linear phase $-k\omega$ is a pure delay by $k$ samples. The **group delay** $\tau_{gd}(\omega)=-\frac{d\angle H_d(\omega)}{d\omega}$ (jumps ignored) generalizes that. If it is constant, every frequency is delayed equally and the waveform survives. If it is not, the frequencies drift apart and the signal smears, even when the magnitude response is perfect.

## 1. Complex numbers: magnitude and the principal angle

Everything in this lecture is the polar form of one complex number at a time, $H_d(\omega_0)$ at each frequency $\omega_0$, so the notes open with a review (more in [[0-toolkit/01-complex-numbers|toolkit: complex numbers]]):

$$
z=a+jb=Re^{j\theta},\qquad |z|=R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=\sqrt{zz^*},\qquad z^*=a-jb=Re^{-j\theta}.
$$

For the angle, $\tan^{-1}(b/a)$ alone only sees the ratio $b/a$, so it cannot tell $1+j$ from $-1-j$ (the notes' Eq. 5 is written this way). Slide 3 fixes the quadrant:

$$
\angle z=\begin{cases}\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right), & a\gt 0\\[4pt] \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)+\pi, & a\lt 0,\ b\gt 0\\[4pt] \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)-\pi, & a\lt 0,\ b\lt 0\end{cases}
$$

The axes, which the slide leaves out, follow by continuity: $\angle z=+\frac{\pi}{2}$ for $a=0,\ b\gt0$, $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ for $a=0,\ b\lt0$, and $\pm\pi$ for a negative real number. This is `np.angle` (atan2), which returns values in $(-\pi,\pi]$.

> [!key] Principal angle and $-1=e^{\pm j\pi}$
> By convention the phase is the **principal angle** in $[-\pi,\pi]$: add or subtract $2\pi$ until it lands there. A minus sign in front of a polar form is not a proper polar form. Absorb it with
> $$
> -1=e^{j\pi}=e^{-j\pi}.
> $$
> The notes' example: $z=-2e^{j\pi/4}=2e^{j\pi/4}e^{-j\pi}=2e^{-j3\pi/4}$. Choosing $e^{+j\pi}$ gives $2e^{j5\pi/4}$, the same number with an angle outside $[-\pi,\pi]$, so pick the sign that keeps you in range. The magnitude is $2$, never $-2$.

> [!question] Quick drill: write each in proper polar form
> (a) $-3e^{-j\pi/3}$ $\qquad$ (b) $-\tfrac12 e^{j5\pi/6}$ $\qquad$ (c) $2j\,e^{-j3\pi/4}$

> [!success]- Answers (checked with `np.angle`)
> (a) $3e^{-j\pi/3}e^{+j\pi}=3e^{j2\pi/3}$ (here $+\pi$ stays in range). (b) $\tfrac12 e^{j5\pi/6}e^{-j\pi}=\tfrac12 e^{-j\pi/6}$. (c) $j=e^{j\pi/2}$, so $2e^{j(\pi/2-3\pi/4)}=2e^{-j\pi/4}$.

## 2. Magnitude response and phase response

[[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]] showed why $H_d(\omega)$ matters ([[concepts/frequency-response|frequency response]]): an LTI system multiplies $e^{j\omega_0 n}$ by the complex number $H_d(\omega_0)$, and for real $h$ it turns $\cos(\omega_0 n+\theta)$ into $|H_d(\omega_0)|\cos\big(\omega_0 n+\theta+\angle H_d(\omega_0)\big)$. The two parts of that complex number get their own names and their own plots.

> [!key] Definitions (notes Eqs. 9–12, slide 4)
> $$
> \lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert=\Big|\sqrt{H_d(\omega)H_d^*(\omega)}\Big|,\qquad \angle H_d(\omega)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{Im}\{H_d(\omega)\}}{\operatorname{Re}\{H_d(\omega)\}}\right)\ \text{(with the quadrant rule)},
> $$
> $$
> H_d(\omega)=|H_d(\omega)|\,e^{j\angle H_d(\omega)} .
> $$
> - The **magnitude response** is never negative, $|H_d(\omega)|\ge 0$. It is the gain at each frequency.
> - The **phase response** is plotted as a principal angle, $-\pi\le\angle H_d(\omega)\le\pi$. When a formula leaves that strip, the plot is moved back by $2\pi$.
> - Both are plotted for $\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]$, since $H_d$ is $2\pi$-periodic. For a **real** $h[n]$, $H_d(-\omega)=H_d^*(\omega)$, so the magnitude is even and the phase is odd ([[concepts/dtft-properties|DTFT properties]]); half the plot determines the other half.

**Linear or dB scale.** A linear plot shows $|H_d(\omega)|$ as it is. A decibel plot shows

$$
\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert_{\mathrm{dB}}=20\log_{10}|H_d(\omega)| ,
$$

so a gain of $1$ is $0$ dB, $2$ is $+6$ dB, $\tfrac12$ is $-6$ dB, $\tfrac1{10}$ is $-20$ dB, and a zero of $H_d$ is $-\infty$ dB. The dB scale is for responses that span several orders of magnitude, like the stopbands of the filters later in the course. For this lecture's examples the linear scale is the right one.

> [!trap] Two kinds of breaks in a phase plot
> - **$2\pi$ wraps** are bookkeeping. $e^{-j2\omega}$ has phase $-2\omega$, a straight line, but inside $[-\pi,\pi]$ it is drawn as a sawtooth that wraps at $\omega=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$. Nothing happens to the signal there.
> - **$\pm\pi$ jumps** are real sign changes. When a real factor of $H_d$ goes from positive to negative, $-1=e^{\pm j\pi}$ moves into the phase. This happens exactly where $|H_d|$ reaches zero *and* the real factor changes sign.
>
> Slide 4 says "changes in sign for $\operatorname{Re}\{H_d(\omega)\}$ lead to $\pm\pi$ jumps". Read that as the sign of the **real amplitude left after the linear-phase factor is pulled out** (§3), not of the real part of $H_d$ itself: $\operatorname{Re}\{e^{-j2\omega}\}=\cos 2\omega$ changes sign four times on $[-\pi,\pi]$, yet $e^{-j2\omega}$ has no $\pi$ jump at all.

## 3. The trick: pull out a linear-phase factor

For a short FIR filter whose taps are symmetric or antisymmetric about their centre $M$, factor $e^{-jM\omega}$ out of $H_d(\omega)=\sum_n h[n]e^{-j\omega n}$. The remaining terms pair up into cosines or sines by Euler's formula. The notes call it "a common trick" (Exercise 1); it is how you get a magnitude and a phase you can sketch by hand.

> [!recipe] Magnitude and phase of a short FIR filter
> 1. Write $H_d(\omega)=\sum_n h[n]e^{-j\omega n}$.
> 2. Factor out $e^{-jM\omega}$, where $M$ is the midpoint of the first and last nonzero samples, and pair the terms: $e^{jk\omega}+e^{-jk\omega}=2\cos k\omega$ and $e^{jk\omega}-e^{-jk\omega}=2j\sin k\omega$.
> 3. Bring the result to the form $H_d(\omega)=e^{j(\alpha-M\omega)}A(\omega)$ with $A(\omega)$ **real**. A factor $j$ is $e^{j\pi/2}$, so $\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}$; a factor $-1$ is $e^{\pm j\pi}$.
> 4. Read off $|H_d(\omega)|=|A(\omega)|$ and
> $$
> \angle H_d(\omega)=\alpha-M\omega+\angle A(\omega),\qquad \angle A(\omega)=\begin{cases}0,& A(\omega)\gt 0\\ \pm\pi,& A(\omega)\lt 0.\end{cases}
> $$
> Then wrap into $[-\pi,\pi]$, choosing the sign of $\pm\pi$ that keeps the plot in range (and odd, for real $h$).
> 5. Sketch: find the zeros of $A$ (where $|H_d|=0$), mark where $A\lt 0$, draw lines of slope $-M$, jump by $\pi$ at each sign change, and wrap at $\pm\pi$.

> [!example] Example 1 (slide 5): $h[n]=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},0,1\}=\delta[n]+\delta[n-2]$
> (a) Determine $H_d(\omega)$, $|H_d(\omega)|$ and $\angle H_d(\omega)$. (b) Plot $|H_d(\omega)|$ and $\angle H_d(\omega)$.

> [!success]- Solution (solved here; there are no annotated slides for Lecture 16)
> The taps are symmetric about $M=1$:
> $$
> H_d(\omega)=1+e^{-j2\omega}=e^{-j\omega}\big(e^{j\omega}+e^{-j\omega}\big)=2e^{-j\omega}\cos\omega .
> $$
> So $A(\omega)=2\cos\omega$ and $\alpha=0$:
> $$
> \lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert=2|\cos\omega|,\qquad \angle H_d(\omega)=-\omega+\angle\cos\omega=\begin{cases}-\omega, & |\omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{2}\\ -\omega+\pi, & \frac{\pi}{2}\lt\omega\le\pi\\ -\omega-\pi, & -\pi\le\omega\lt-\frac{\pi}{2}.\end{cases}
> $$
> The magnitude is $2$ at $\omega=0$ and at $\pm\pi$, and $0$ at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$, where $H(z)=1+z^{-2}$ has its zeros $z=\pm j$. The phase is a line of slope $-1$, jumps by $\pi$ at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ where $\cos\omega$ changes sign, and stays inside $[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]$. It is back at $0$ at $\omega=\pm\pi$ because $H_d(\pi)=2e^{-j\pi}\cos\pi=+2$. (Checked against $\sum_n h[n]e^{-j\omega n}$ on a fine grid; Figure 1, top.)

> [!example] Example 2 (slide 7) = notes Exercise 1: $h[n]=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},0,-1\}=\delta[n]-\delta[n-2]$
> Same two questions.

> [!success]- Solution
> The taps are antisymmetric about $M=1$ (notes Eqs. 14–20):
> $$
> H_d(\omega)=1-e^{-j2\omega}=e^{-j\omega}\big(e^{j\omega}-e^{-j\omega}\big)=2je^{-j\omega}\sin\omega=2e^{j(\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega)}\sin\omega .
> $$
> So $A(\omega)=2\sin\omega$ and $\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}$:
> $$
> \lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert=2|\sin\omega|,\qquad \angle H_d(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega+\angle\sin\omega=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega, & 0\lt\omega\le\pi\\ -\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega, & -\pi\le\omega\lt 0.\end{cases}
> $$
> The zeros are at $\omega=0$ and $\pm\pi$ ($H(z)=1-z^{-2}$ has zeros $z=\pm1$). On $(-\pi,0)$ the sine is negative, so the phase picks up $-\pi$. It runs from $\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $\omega=-\pi$ down to $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ just left of $0$, jumps by $\pi$ at $\omega=0$, and runs from $\frac{\pi}{2}$ down to $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $\omega=\pi$, as in the notes' Fig. 1. > [!trap] The sign belongs to the phase, not to the magnitude
> "$|H_d(\omega)|=2\sin\omega$" is wrong for $\omega\lt 0$: a magnitude is never negative (slide 4: "Remember that magnitude is always non-negative!"). Write $2|\sin\omega|$ and move the sign into the phase as $\pm\pi$. The opposite slip is as common: dropping the $\pm\pi$ and drawing Example 2's phase as the single line $\frac{\pi}{2}-\omega$ across $[-\pi,\pi]$. That line is off by $\pi$ on all of $(-\pi,0)$ and even leaves the strip $[-\pi,\pi]$ for $\omega\lt-\frac{\pi}{2}$.

## 4. When no real factor comes out: Example 3

> [!example] Example 3 (slides 8–9) = notes Exercise 2: $h[n]=\{\underset{\uparrow}{3},2,1\}=3\delta[n]+2\delta[n-1]+\delta[n-2]$
> Determine and plot $H_d(\omega)$, $|H_d(\omega)|$ and $\angle H_d(\omega)$.

> [!success]- Solution
> $$
> H_d(\omega)=3+2e^{-j\omega}+e^{-j2\omega} .
> $$
> Pulling out $e^{-j\omega}$ no longer helps: $e^{j\omega}H_d(\omega)=(2+4\cos\omega)+j\,2\sin\omega$ is still complex, because $\{3,2,1\}$ is not symmetric. So go back to the definitions (notes Eqs. 24–27):
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert^2&=\big(3+2e^{-j\omega}+e^{-j2\omega}\big)\big(3+2e^{j\omega}+e^{j2\omega}\big)\\
> &=14+8e^{j\omega}+8e^{-j\omega}+3e^{j2\omega}+3e^{-j2\omega}=14+16\cos\omega+6\cos2\omega ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $$
> \angle H_d(\omega)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-2\sin\omega-\sin2\omega}{3+2\cos\omega+\cos2\omega}\right).
> $$
> The real part $3+2\cos\omega+\cos2\omega=2(\cos^2\omega+\cos\omega+1)$ is never below $\frac32$, so $H_d$ stays in the right half-plane and the plain $\tan^{-1}$ is the right branch everywhere.
>
> Key values: $|H_d(0)|=6$; $|H_d(\pm\frac{\pi}{2})|=2\sqrt2$, with $H_d(\frac{\pi}{2})=2-2j$ and phase $-\frac{\pi}{4}$; $|H_d(\pm\pi)|=2$ with phase $0$. The magnitude is not monotone: with $c=\cos\omega$, $|H_d|^2=4(3c^2+4c+2)$ is smallest at $c=-\frac23$, where $|H_d|=\sqrt{8/3}\approx1.63$ ($\omega\approx\pm0.73\pi$). The phase is smooth and odd, and never goes beyond $\pm\frac{\pi}{4}$, reached at $\omega=\mp\frac{\pi}{2}$ (notes Fig. 2, slide 9). (All values checked numerically.)

> [!note] Why Example 3 has no jumps
> The notes say the phase has no $\pm\pi$ jumps "since the expression inside the absolute value signs never goes negative". That expression, $14+16\cos\omega+6\cos2\omega$, is $|H_d(\omega)|^2$, which is never negative for *any* system, so it cannot be the reason. The reason is that $H_d(\omega)$ never passes through $0$: its real part never drops below $\frac32$ (Figure 2, right). In z-domain terms, the zeros of $H(z)=3+2z^{-1}+z^{-2}$ are $\frac{-1\pm j\sqrt2}{3}$, with $|z|=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\lt 1$. No zero sits on the unit circle, so there is no frequency where $|H_d|=0$ and nothing for a sign to flip through.

> [!key] Where the $\pm\pi$ jumps are
> A phase jump of $\pm\pi$ at $\omega_0$ needs $H_d(\omega_0)=0$ with a sign change: **a zero of $H(z)$ on the unit circle at $z=e^{j\omega_0}$**, of odd multiplicity. Examples 1 and 2 have zeros $\pm j$ and $\pm1$ on the circle and jump there; Example 3's zeros are inside, and its phase is continuous. A double zero on the circle makes $|H_d|$ touch $0$ without a jump: concept check $H_5$ below, and [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2]] #3.

## 5. Concept check: which phases jump by π?

> [!question] Slide 6 — which of these have $\pm\pi$ jump discontinuities in their phase response? (Select all that apply.)
> 1. $H_1(\omega)=\cos(4\omega)$
> 2. $H_2(\omega)=e^{-j2\omega}\big(2+\cos(2\omega)\big)$
> 3. $H_3(\omega)=je^{-j3\omega}\sin(3\omega)$
> 4. $H_4(\omega)=e^{-j2\omega}\big(1-2\cos(\omega)-\cos(2\omega)\big)$
> 5. $H_5(\omega)=e^{-j2\omega}\big(3-2\cos(\omega)-\cos(2\omega)\big)$

> [!success]- Answer: $H_1$, $H_3$ and $H_4$ (solved here; checked by counting the jumps of `np.angle` on a fine grid)
> Each one is already written as (linear phase) × (real amplitude), so only the sign of the real amplitude matters.
> - **$H_1$: yes.** $\cos4\omega$ is real and changes sign at $\omega=\pm\frac{\pi}{8},\pm\frac{3\pi}{8},\pm\frac{5\pi}{8},\pm\frac{7\pi}{8}$. The phase alternates between $0$ and $\pm\pi$; a real $H_d$ has no other phase values.
> - **$H_2$: no.** $2+\cos2\omega\ge1\gt0$. The phase is $-2\omega$, with $2\pi$ wraps at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$: wraps, not jumps.
> - **$H_3$: yes.** $\sin3\omega$ changes sign at $\omega=0,\pm\frac{\pi}{3},\pm\frac{2\pi}{3}$. The factor $j$ only adds a constant $\frac{\pi}{2}$.
> - **$H_4$: yes.** With $\cos2\omega=2\cos^2\omega-1$, the real factor is $1-2\cos\omega-\cos2\omega=-2(\cos^2\omega+\cos\omega-1)$. It is $-2$ at $\omega=0$ and $+2$ at $\pm\pi$, and changes sign at $\cos\omega=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}\approx0.618$, i.e. $\omega\approx\pm0.29\pi$.
> - **$H_5$: no.** The real factor is $3-2\cos\omega-\cos2\omega=2(1-\cos\omega)(2+\cos\omega)\ge0$. It *touches* $0$ at $\omega=0$, so $|H_5(0)|=0$, but it never changes sign, and the phase $-2\omega$ runs straight through.
>
> $H_5$ is the trap: a zero of the magnitude is not enough, the real amplitude has to cross zero. The [[concepts/magnitude-and-phase-response|magnitude and phase response]] page draws $H_4$ and $H_5$ side by side.

## 6. What the phase does: a linear phase is a delay

Slides 10–11 isolate the phase with a system whose magnitude is $1$ at every frequency.

> [!question] Slides 10–11 — $H_d(\omega)=e^{-j2\omega}$, so $|H_d(\omega)|=1$ and $\angle H_d(\omega)=-2\omega$
> What is the response to (a) $x[n]=e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}$, (b) $x[n]=e^{j\frac{3\pi}{4}n}$, (c) $x[n]=e^{j\omega_0 n}$? (d) What is the response to $e^{j\omega_0n}$ when $H_d(\omega)=e^{-jk\omega}$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$?

> [!success]- Answers (the eigenfunction property of Lecture 15: $e^{j\omega_0n}\mapsto H_d(\omega_0)\,e^{j\omega_0n}$)
> (a) $H_d(\frac{\pi}{2})=e^{-j\pi}=-1$, so $y[n]=-e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}n}=e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}(n-2)}$.
> (b) $H_d(\frac{3\pi}{4})=e^{-j\frac{3\pi}{2}}=j$, so $y[n]=j\,e^{j\frac{3\pi}{4}n}=e^{j\frac{3\pi}{4}(n-2)}$. The principal angle of $H_d(\frac{3\pi}{4})$ is $+\frac{\pi}{2}$, which *looks* like an advance; the unwrapped phase $-\frac{3\pi}{2}$ shows it is the same two-sample delay.
> (c) $y[n]=e^{-j2\omega_0}e^{j\omega_0n}=e^{j\omega_0(n-2)}=x[n-2]$. Every frequency is delayed by exactly two samples, so every input is: this is the system $h[n]=\delta[n-2]$.
> (d) $y[n]=e^{-jk\omega_0}e^{j\omega_0n}=e^{j\omega_0(n-k)}=x[n-k]$, a delay by $k$. This is the DTFT delay property $x[n-k]\leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega k}X_d(\omega)$ read backwards: **a linear phase $-k\omega$ is a pure delay of $k$ samples.**

For a real sinusoid it is one line: through $e^{-jk\omega}$, $\cos(\omega_0n+\theta)\mapsto\cos(\omega_0n+\theta-k\omega_0)=\cos\big(\omega_0(n-k)+\theta\big)$. The phase shift $-k\omega_0$ is *proportional to the frequency*, and that is exactly what shifts every component by the same $k$ samples. A constant phase shift $\theta_0$ at every frequency would not: it moves a cosine of frequency $\omega_0$ by $\theta_0/\omega_0$ samples, a lot for slow components and little for fast ones.

## 7. Group delay

The delay property says a shift by $k$ samples adds the linear phase $f(\omega)=-k\omega$, whose slope is $-k$ (notes Eqs. 28–29). The lecture turns this around and *defines* the delay at each frequency as minus the slope of the phase.

> [!key] Group delay (notes Eq. 30, slide 12)
> $$
> \tau_{gd}(\omega)=-\frac{d\angle H_d(\omega)}{d\omega}\qquad\text{(in samples)}
> $$
> Differentiate the **continuous** phase: ignore the $\pm\pi$ jumps and the $2\pi$ wraps. Between them the slope is well defined; at a jump the derivative does not exist and that single point is left out (the notes: the derivative is "$-1$ almost everywhere").
> - **Linear phase**, $\angle H_d(\omega)=\alpha-k\omega$ (with $\pm\pi$ jumps allowed): $\tau_{gd}(\omega)=k$ for every $\omega$, a **uniform group delay**. Every frequency component is delayed by the same $k$ samples, so the waveform comes out intact, only shifted.
> - **Non-linear phase**: $\tau_{gd}(\omega)$ changes with $\omega$, a **non-uniform group delay**. Different frequency components are delayed by different amounts, and the signal loses its shape.

The three examples:

- **Examples 1 and 2.** The phase is a line of slope $-1$ broken by $\pi$ jumps, so $\tau_{gd}(\omega)=1$: every frequency is delayed by one sample, the centre of the three taps. The notes make this point for Exercise 1: $\angle H_d$ is not differentiable at $\omega=0$, but its derivative is $-1$ everywhere else.
- **A pure delay** $e^{-jk\omega}$: $\tau_{gd}=k$, the case that motivated the definition.
- **Example 3.** The phase is smooth but curved, so the group delay varies with frequency (derivation below): $\frac23$ of a sample at $\omega=0$, zero at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$, and negative in between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$.

> [!derivation]- Example 3's group delay, from the arctan
> With $\angle H_d=\tan^{-1}(I/R)$, $R=3+2\cos\omega+\cos2\omega$ and $I=-(2\sin\omega+\sin2\omega)$, the chain rule gives $\frac{d}{d\omega}\tan^{-1}\frac{I}{R}=\frac{RI'-IR'}{R^2+I^2}$, so
> $$
> \tau_{gd}(\omega)=\frac{IR'-RI'}{R^2+I^2}=\frac{6+12\cos\omega+6\cos2\omega}{14+16\cos\omega+6\cos2\omega}=\frac{3c(1+c)}{3c^2+4c+2},\qquad c=\cos\omega .
> $$
> (With $\cos2\omega=2c^2-1$ the numerator is $12c(1+c)$ and the denominator $4(3c^2+4c+2)$.) So $\tau_{gd}(0)=\frac69=\frac23$: low frequencies come out two-thirds of a sample late, which is the "centre of mass" $\frac{0\cdot3+1\cdot2+2\cdot1}{3+2+1}=\frac23$ of $h$. At $\omega=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pm\pi$ the group delay is $0$, and for $\frac{\pi}{2}\lt|\omega|\lt\pi$ it is **negative**, down to $-\frac{3\sqrt2}{4}\approx-1.06$ at $\cos\omega=\sqrt2-2$ ($\omega\approx\pm0.70\pi$). Negative values are allowed (the filter is still causal); they only say that the phase rises with $\omega$ there, as Figure 2 shows. Checked against `scipy.signal.group_delay` and against a finite-difference derivative of the unwrapped phase.

**Non-uniform group delay in pictures (notes Fig. 3, slides 13–15).** The lecture's illustration is a noisy grayscale photo, a corgi resting its head on a book (*The Art of Raising a Puppy*). It is filtered by two 2-D LTI systems with the **same** low-pass magnitude response, close to $1$ for low spatial frequencies and close to $0$ above roughly $\frac{\pi}{2}$, where most of the noise lives. Both remove the same noise; only their phases differ.

- System 1 has a **linear phase** (Fig. 3e: straight segments of one slope, with $2\pi$ wraps in the passband and $\pi$ jumps where the small stopband ripples change sign), so its group delay is uniform. Its output (Fig. 3b) is clean and sharp, only shifted by a few pixels in $x$ and $y$: there is a dark band along the top and left edges.
- System 2 has a phase that **oscillates sinusoidally** in $\omega$ (Fig. 3f, about eight oscillations between $0$ and $\pi$), so its group delay swings between large positive and negative values. Its output (Fig. 3c) contains the same frequencies with the same strengths, yet it is ruined by "shaking" and ringing artifacts: ghost copies of edges and streaks across the picture. Different spatial frequencies (cycles per pixel) were moved by different amounts, and the image lost its coherence.

The lesson for filter design, which the course returns to later: the magnitude response decides *which* frequencies survive, the phase decides *where* they end up. For pictures and waveforms you want (close to) linear phase. Why a symmetric FIR filter delivers exactly that, and why a sinusoidal phase makes ghosts, is on the [[concepts/group-delay|group delay and linear phase]] page.

**In Python.** `freqz` evaluates $H_d$ at chosen frequencies, `np.angle` gives the principal phase, `np.unwrap` removes the $2\pi$ wraps, and `scipy.signal.group_delay` computes $\tau_{gd}$ directly:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import freqz, group_delay

h = [3, 2, 1]                                   # Example 3 (notes: Exercise 2)
w = np.array([0, np.pi/2, 0.7*np.pi, np.pi])
_, H = freqz(h, [1], worN=w)                    # H_d(w) = sum h[n] e^{-jwn}
_, tau = group_delay((h, [1]), w=w)             # -d(phase)/dw
print("w/pi       :", w / np.pi)
print("|H|        :", np.round(np.abs(H), 3))
print("phase/pi   :", np.round(np.angle(H) / np.pi, 3))
print("group delay:", np.round(tau, 3))

# a pure delay: the principal phase hides the straight line, np.unwrap restores it
w = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 9)                    # steps of pi/8
_, H = freqz([0, 0, 1], [1], worN=w)            # h = delta[n-2], H = e^{-j2w}
print("angle/pi   :", np.round(np.angle(H) / np.pi, 2))
print("unwrap/pi  :", np.round(np.unwrap(np.angle(H)) / np.pi, 2))
```

The first block reproduces Example 3: $6$, $2\sqrt2$ and $2$, the phase $-\frac{\pi}{4}$ at $\frac{\pi}{2}$, and the group delay $\frac23$, $0$, about $-1.06$ (near its minimum) and $0$. In the second block the principal phase of $e^{-j2\omega}$ reaches $-\pi$ at $\omega=\frac{\pi}{2}$ and restarts at $+0.75\pi$; `np.unwrap` adds $2\pi$ back and recovers the line $-2\omega$, whose slope is the delay. `np.unwrap` only removes jumps larger than $\pi$, so it does not reliably straighten the $\pm\pi$ jumps of Examples 1–2; `group_delay` is computed from the coefficients and does not care.

> [!question] Practice — three short filters
> For each, find $|H_d(\omega)|$, $\angle H_d(\omega)$, the $\pm\pi$ jumps and the group delay: (a) $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},2,1\}$ $\quad$ (b) $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{2},0,0,2\}$ $\quad$ (c) $h=\{\underset{\uparrow}{1},-1\}$.

> [!success]- Solutions (checked numerically)
> **(a)** $H_d=e^{-j\omega}\big(e^{j\omega}+2+e^{-j\omega}\big)=e^{-j\omega}(2+2\cos\omega)$, and $2+2\cos\omega\ge0$. So $|H_d|=2+2\cos\omega$ ($4$ at $\omega=0$, $2$ at $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$, $0$ at $\pm\pi$), $\angle H_d=-\omega$, and there are no $\pi$ jumps: the zero at $\omega=\pm\pi$ is a double zero, since $H(z)=(1+z^{-1})^2$. $\tau_{gd}=1$.
>
> **(b)** $M=\frac32$: $H_d=2e^{-j\frac32\omega}\big(e^{j\frac32\omega}+e^{-j\frac32\omega}\big)=4e^{-j\frac32\omega}\cos\frac{3\omega}{2}$. So $|H_d|=4|\cos\frac{3\omega}{2}|$, with zeros at $\pm\frac{\pi}{3}$ and $\pm\pi$ and back to $4$ at $\pm\frac{2\pi}{3}$. The phase is $-\frac32\omega$ for $|\omega|\lt\frac{\pi}{3}$ and $-\frac32\omega\pm\pi$ for $\frac{\pi}{3}\lt|\omega|\lt\pi$: $\pi$ jumps at $\pm\frac{\pi}{3}$ (the zero at $\pm\pi$ sits on the edge of the plot). $\tau_{gd}=\frac32$: a half-sample group delay is fine, even though no sample of $h$ sits at $n=\frac32$.
>
> **(c)** $H_d=1-e^{-j\omega}=e^{-j\omega/2}\big(e^{j\omega/2}-e^{-j\omega/2}\big)=2e^{j(\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\omega}{2})}\sin\frac{\omega}{2}$. So $|H_d|=2|\sin\frac{\omega}{2}|$, a high-pass ($0$ at DC, $2$ at $\pm\pi$). The phase is $\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\omega}{2}$ for $0\lt\omega\le\pi$ and $-\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\omega}{2}$ for $-\pi\le\omega\lt0$: one $\pi$ jump, at $\omega=0$. $\tau_{gd}=\frac12$.
