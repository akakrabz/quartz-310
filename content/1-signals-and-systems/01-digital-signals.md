> [!abstract] In one breath
> A signal is any measurable quantity that varies over time, space or another ordering variable. A continuous-time signal $x(t)$ exists for every real $t$; a discrete-time signal $x[n]$ exists only at integers $n$. Sampling every $T$ seconds connects them: $x[n] = x(nT)$. Quantizing the values as well gives a *digital* signal, which computers can store, copy and process exactly. The lecture itself is qualitative; what the rest of the course (and HW1) silently assumes is the ability to handle sequences: know where $n = 0$ is, write signals with $\delta[n]$ and $u[n]$, and transform the index correctly. For $x[an+b]$ the safe rule is **shift first, then flip or compress**, or simply evaluate the index sample by sample.

## 1. What is a signal?

> [!key] Signal
> A **signal** is any measurable quantity that varies over time, space, or any other variable that orders or organizes the measurements.

The notes' examples cover the range: an **audio** signal (acoustic pressure versus time, e.g. the word "Goodbye"), a **financial** signal (a price versus time: not physical, still a signal), an **image** (three color intensities at each integer pixel coordinate $(x, y)$; a video adds time), and a **graph signal** (values attached to the vertices of a network, such as traffic at road intersections; beyond this course). This course works with one-dimensional sequences $x[n]$, but everything generalizes.

## 2. Continuous-time versus discrete-time, analog versus digital

- A **continuous-time** signal $x(t)$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$, is observed at every instant: any interval contains infinitely (uncountably) many values. Parentheses and $t$.
- A **discrete-time** signal $x[n]$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, is observed at regularly spaced instants; the time axis is *discretized*. Brackets and $n$.

**Sampling** turns the first into the second. With **sampling period** $T$ (sampling rate $f_s = 1/T$),

$$
x[n] = x(nT), \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

Music is typically recorded at $f_s = 44.1$ kHz: $44{,}100$ samples per second, one every $T = 1/44100 \approx 2.268\times10^{-5}$ s $\approx 22.7\ \mu$s.

The **values** can be continuous or discrete as well:

| | continuous-valued | discrete-valued (quantized) |
|---|---|---|
| **continuous-time** | **analog** signal | e.g. a logic-level waveform |
| **discrete-time** | discrete-time signal | **digital** signal |

A computer stores numbers with a fixed number of bits, so real digital signals are quantized. In ECE 310 the values are treated as continuous, because floating-point precision makes quantization negligible for our purposes; "digital signal" and "discrete-time signal" are used interchangeably from here on.

> [!note] Why process signals digitally?
> 1. **Mathematics is easy:** multiplication, exponentials, matrix operations and square roots are one line of code, and nearly impossible with analog circuits.
> 2. **Reliability:** digital hardware does not drift with temperature or age, is exactly repeatable, and can correct its own errors.
> 3. **Storage:** one vinyl record holds an album; a thumbnail-sized chip holds tens of thousands of songs, and images and text besides.
> 4. **Flexibility:** changing a digital system means changing numbers in code, not ordering new parts.
>
> Analog signals remain essential (power electronics, acoustics, RF), and many systems are mixed-signal. Sampling and reconstruction in detail (aliasing, A/D and D/A conversion) come later in the course, after Midterm 1.

## 3. Sequences: where is $n = 0$?

A list of numbers is not yet a signal: you must say which sample sits at $n = 0$. This site (like the course) puts an arrow under it: $x[n] = \{2, 4, \underset{\uparrow}{1}, 0, -2, 5, 7, 3\}$ means $x[-2] = 2$, $x[-1] = 4$, $x[0] = 1$, …, $x[5] = 3$, and $x[n] = 0$ outside $-2 \le n \le 5$. The two building blocks (defined with the other elementary signals in [[1-signals-and-systems/02-complex-numbers-and-elementary-signals|Lecture 2]]):

$$
\delta[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n = 0\\ 0, & n \ne 0\end{cases}
\qquad
u[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n \ge 0\\ 0, & n < 0\end{cases}
$$

A sample of $\delta[n - k]$ or the edge of $u[n - k]$ sits where the **argument is zero**, at $n = k$. Products and differences of steps cut out windows: $u[n+2] - u[n-3]$ is $1$ on $-2 \le n \le 2$, and $u[n]\,u[n-4] = u[n-4]$ (both must be on; [[homework/hw1|HW1]] #1(c)).

## 4. Transforming the time index

Three elementary operations act on the index only; the sample **values** never change:

- **shift** $x[n - n_0]$: moves the signal **right** by $n_0$ (a delay) if $n_0 > 0$, left if $n_0 < 0$. Check: the sample that was at $n = 0$ now appears where $n - n_0 = 0$, i.e. at $n = n_0$;
- **reversal** $x[-n]$: mirrors the signal about $n = 0$;
- **compression** (downsampling) $x[Mn]$, $M$ a positive integer: keeps every $M$-th sample and **discards** the rest.

> [!key] Order of operations for $y[n] = x[an + b]$
> - **Shift first, then flip/compress:** $v[n] = x[n + b]$, then $y[n] = v[an]$. Check: $v[an] = x[an + b]$ ✓.
> - **Flip/compress first, then shift by $b/a$, not by $b$:** $w[n] = x[an]$, then $y[n] = w\!\left[n + \tfrac{b}{a}\right] = x[a(n + \tfrac{b}{a})]$. For $x[-n+3] = x[-(n-3)]$ this means: flip, then shift **right** by 3. When $b/a$ is not an integer (e.g. $x[2n+1]$), this order is impossible; shift first.
> - **Always safe:** evaluate the index. For each $n$, compute $m = an + b$ and read off $x[m]$.

> [!question] [[homework/hw1|HW1]] #2: with $x[n] = \{2, 4, \underset{\uparrow}{1}, 0, -2, 5, 7, 3\}$, find $y[n] = x[-n+3]$ and $z[n] = x[2n+1]$.

> [!success]- Answers (verified)
> **$y[n] = x[-n+3]$.** Index table: $n = -2 \to x[5] = 3$; $n = -1 \to x[4] = 7$; $n = 0 \to x[3] = 5$; $n = 1 \to x[2] = -2$; $n = 2 \to x[1] = 0$; $n = 3 \to x[0] = 1$; $n = 4 \to x[-1] = 4$; $n = 5 \to x[-2] = 2$. So
> $$
> y[n] = \{3,\ 7,\ \underset{\uparrow}{5},\ -2,\ 0,\ 1,\ 4,\ 2\}, \qquad -2 \le n \le 5 .
> $$
> Same result by the rule: shift left by 3, $v[n] = x[n+3] = \{2, 4, 1, 0, -2, \underset{\uparrow}{5}, 7, 3\}$, then flip $y[n] = v[-n]$.
> **$z[n] = x[2n+1]$.** The argument $2n + 1$ is always odd: $n = -1 \to x[-1] = 4$; $n = 0 \to x[1] = 0$; $n = 1 \to x[3] = 5$; $n = 2 \to x[5] = 3$; everything else reads outside the support. So
> $$
> z[n] = \{4,\ \underset{\uparrow}{0},\ 5,\ 3\}, \qquad -1 \le n \le 2,
> $$
> and $x[-2], x[0], x[2], x[4]$ are lost. By the rule: shift left by 1, $v[n] = x[n+1]$, then keep the even-indexed samples $v[2n]$.

Middle: y[n] = x[−n+3] reads x at index 3 − n, so y[0] = x[3] = 5, y[−2] = x[5] = 3 and y[5] = x[−2] = 2; the whole signal is reversed and re-centred. Bottom: z[n] = x[2n+1] reads only odd indices, z[−1] = x[−1] = 4, z[0] = x[1] = 0, z[1] = x[3] = 5, z[2] = x[5] = 3; the even-indexed samples x[−2], x[0], x[2], x[4] are gone for good.</figcaption></figure>

> [!trap] Flip-then-shift in the wrong direction
> Flipping $x[n]$ and then shifting **left** by 3 gives $x[-(n+3)] = x[-n-3]$, not $x[-n+3]$. Likewise, compressing first and then shifting by 1 gives $x[2(n+1)] = x[2n+2]$, not $x[2n+1]$. If in doubt, check one sample: $y[0]$ must equal $x[b]$ ($= x[3]$ here), and $z[0] = x[1]$.

The index table is also what a program does. A dictionary keyed by $n$ keeps the bookkeeping honest:

```python
x = {-2: 2, -1: 4, 0: 1, 1: 0, 2: -2, 3: 5, 4: 7, 5: 3}    # HW1 #2: x[n], zero elsewhere
y = {n: x.get(-n + 3, 0) for n in range(-4, 8)}            # y[n] = x[-n+3]: look up index -n+3
z = {n: x.get(2*n + 1, 0) for n in range(-4, 8)}           # z[n] = x[2n+1]: look up index 2n+1
print("y[-2..5]:", [y[n] for n in range(-2, 6)])
print("z[-1..2]:", [z[n] for n in range(-1, 3)])
```

```text
y[-2..5]: [3, 7, 5, -2, 0, 1, 4, 2]
z[-1..2]: [4, 0, 5, 3]
```
