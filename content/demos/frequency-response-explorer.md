**How to type a system.** For an FIR filter, list the taps $h[n_0], h[n_0+1], \dots$ in **b**, leave **a** = `1`, and put the index of the first tap in **n₀**. So $h[n] = \{\underset{\uparrow}{3}, 2, 1\}$ is b = `3, 2, 1`, n₀ = `0`, and the centred moving average $\tfrac13\{1, \underset{\uparrow}{1}, 1\}$ is b = `1/3, 1/3, 1/3`, n₀ = `-1`. For a recursive system use the [[2-z-transform/09-transfer-functions|Lecture 9]] coefficients, $y[n] + \sum_{k\ge1} a_k y[n-k] = \sum_k b_k x[n-k]$ (the `b, a` of `scipy.signal.lfilter`). The demo then plots

$$
H_d(\omega) = e^{-j\omega n_0}\,\frac{\sum_k b_k e^{-j\omega k}}{\sum_k a_k e^{-j\omega k}},
$$

which is $H(z)\big|_{z=e^{j\omega}}$ when the ROC contains the unit circle. The **poles & zeros** mode takes the roots instead (`0.9e^(jπ/3)`, `-1`; the conjugate of a complex root is added for you, so $h[n]$ stays real) and a gain $b[0]$. Dragging a marker on the z-plane switches to that mode. Fractions, `sqrt(2)/2` and `e^(j2pi/3)` are accepted everywhere.

> [!exam] How this is tested
> Midterm 2 asks you to sketch $|H_d(\omega)|$ and $\angle H_d(\omega)$ for a short FIR filter ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2023|SP2023 MT2 #5]], [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2025|SP2025 MT2 #3]], [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2 #3]] (the magnitude only), [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/spring-2021|SP2021 MT2 #2–3]]) and to push a sum of sinusoids through a system ([[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2024|FA2024 MT2 #4]]; [[exams/midterm-2/past-exams/fall-2023|FA2023 MT2 #4]] uses a complex $h[n]$ on purpose, which this demo does not handle). SP2021 #3, SP2023 #5 and FA2024 #3 are presets; for SP2025 #3 type b = `1, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 1`. Use the demo to check a sketch after you have drawn it: on the exam you get no plot, and the labels on your axes (zeros, peak values, the size of each jump) are what earn the points. The recipes are in [[problems/magnitude-phase-and-group-delay]] and [[problems/lti-response-to-sinusoids]].

## What to try

1. **A magnitude, a phase and a π jump: Lecture 16, Exercise 1.** The first preset is $h[n] = \delta[n] - \delta[n-2]$, so $H_d(\omega) = 1 - e^{-j2\omega} = 2j\,e^{-j\omega}\sin\omega$. The magnitude is $2\lvert\sin\omega\rvert$: zero at $\omega = 0$ and $\pm\pi$, peak $2$ at $\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. The phase is $\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \omega$ for $\omega > 0$ and $-\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \omega$ for $\omega < 0$. It jumps by $\pi$ at $\omega = 0$, exactly where the magnitude touches zero, because $\sin\omega$ changes sign there and $-1 = e^{\pm j\pi}$. The dotted connector marks the jump; it is not part of the curve. The group delay is $1$ at every $\omega$ where it is defined, as the notes say: the jump changes the phase but not its slope. Set ω₀ = π/2. Then $H_d(\tfrac{\pi}{2}) = 2$ and the output is $2\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}n)$, in step with the input, even though the group delay is $1$. The constant $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ from the $j$ shifts the phase without changing its slope, so the *phase* delay $0$ and the *group* delay $1$ are different numbers here.
2. **No jumps, but not linear either: Exercise 2, $\{3, 2, 1\}$.** Now $\lvert H_d\rvert^2 = 14 + 16\cos\omega + 6\cos 2\omega$ never reaches zero (smallest value $\sqrt{8/3} \approx 1.63$, near $\omega \approx 2.30$), so the phase has no jumps. Its slope still changes: the group delay falls from $\tfrac23$ at $\omega = 0$ to about $-1.06$ near $\omega \approx 2.2$ and comes back to $0$ at $\pi$. That is the notes' "non-uniform group delay": different frequencies come out shifted by different amounts. At ω₀ = π/3 the readout gives $\lvert H_d\rvert = \sqrt{19}$, matching $14 + 8 - 3 = 19$ from the formula.
3. **Moving the arrow only adds linear phase: slides Example 1, $\{1, 0, 1\}$.** The causal version is $H_d(\omega) = 2\cos\omega\cdot e^{-j\omega}$: zeros at $\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ with a π jump at each, and group delay $1$. Now change n₀ to `-1`. The magnitude plot does not move at all, the phase collapses to $0$ or $\pm\pi$ ($H_d = 2\cos\omega$ is real), and the group delay drops to $0$. That is the time-shift property of [[3-fourier-analysis/14-dtft-properties|Lecture 14]], $x[n-k] \leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega k}X_d(\omega)$, seen from the plot.
4. **The phase is a delay: "what does the phase do?" from the Lecture 16 slides.** Pick *Pure delay*, $H_d(\omega) = e^{-j2\omega}$. The magnitude is flat at $1$. The principal phase $-2\omega$ runs off the bottom of $[-\pi, \pi]$ and wraps by $2\pi$ at $\omega = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. Tick *unwrap* and it becomes one straight line of slope $-2$. At ω₀ = π/2, $H_d = e^{-j\pi} = -1$, so the output is $-\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}n) = \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}(n-2))$. At ω₀ = 3π/4, $H_d = e^{-j3\pi/2} = j$: the principal phase reads $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, and $\cos(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}n + \tfrac{\pi}{2}) = \cos(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}(n-2))$ is still the input two samples later. The phase-delay readout uses the unwrapped phase, so it says $2$ at every ω₀.
5. **What a moving average removes (Lectures 3, 5 and 15).** Pick the causal 3-point average. It is a low-pass filter, $\lvert H_d(\omega)\rvert = \tfrac13\lvert 1 + 2\cos\omega\rvert$, with a zero at $\omega = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}$. Set ω₀ = 2pi/3: the output is identically zero, and the direct-check circles (the convolution sum computed sample by sample) sit on the axis too. A cosine at $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$ has period 3, so every window of three samples sums to zero. Then pick the centred version: same magnitude, but $H_d(\omega) = \tfrac13(1 + 2\cos\omega)$ is real. At ω₀ = 5π/6 it is $\tfrac{1-\sqrt3}{3} \approx -0.244$, so the output is $0.244\cos(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}n + \pi)$, an upside-down copy.
6. **A pole makes a peak (the first-order system $h[n] = a^n u[n]$ of Lecture 14).** Pick $H(z) = 1/(1 - 0.8z^{-1})$ and tick *dB*. The peak is $\lvert H_d(0)\rvert = 5$ ($13.98$ dB), falling to $\tfrac59$ ($-5.11$ dB) at $\omega = \pi$, and the group delay is $4$ samples at $\omega = 0$. Drag the pole to the right (or switch to *poles & zeros* and type `0.95`): at $0.95$ the peak is $20$ ($26.02$ dB) and much narrower. The high-pass preset puts the pole at $-0.8$ and the peak at $\omega = \pi$. The explorer refuses to drag a pole onto or past the unit circle, because the causal system would be unstable and its DTFT would not exist. If you *type* such a pole, a warning explains what the curves mean.
7. **Zeros on the circle are notches.** Pick *Notch*: zeros at $e^{\pm j\pi/3}$ give $H_d(\omega) = e^{-j\omega}(2\cos\omega - 1)$, so the cosine at $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ is blocked exactly ($\lvert H_d\rvert = 1$ at $\omega = 0$, $3$ at $\pi$). Drag a zero slightly off the circle and the null becomes a dip. *Sharp notch* adds poles at $0.9e^{\pm j\pi/3}$ right behind the zeros. Away from $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ the pole and zero almost cancel, and the response is nearly flat ($1.099$ at $\omega = 0$, $1.107$ at $\pi$). This is the pole-zero intuition that later lectures build filters with.
8. **Past exams.** *SP2021 #3* is $x[n] = 3\delta[n+1] - 3\delta[n-7]$, whose DTFT the key writes as $6j\,e^{-j3\omega}\sin 4\omega$. Antisymmetric taps about $n = 3$ give group delay $3$ and $\lvert X_d\rvert = 6\lvert\sin 4\omega\rvert$. *SP2023 #5* is $\delta[n] + \delta[n-6]$: $\lvert H_d\rvert = 2\lvert\cos 3\omega\rvert$, with zeros and π jumps at $\pm\tfrac{\pi}{6}, \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \pm\tfrac{5\pi}{6}$. *FA2024 #3* is $\delta[n+2] + \delta[n] + \delta[n-2]$, with $H_d(\omega) = 1 + 2\cos 2\omega$. The key's values $H_d(0) = 3$, $H_d(\tfrac{\pi}{2}) = -1$, $H_d(\pi) = 3$ are the readouts at those ω₀; at $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ the phase reads $\pi$ because the value is negative.

> [!tip] Reading a sketch off the formula
> When the taps are symmetric, the system card shows $H_d(\omega) = e^{-jM\omega}A(\omega)$ with $A$ real (antisymmetric: $j\,e^{-jM\omega}A(\omega)$). Then $\lvert H_d\rvert = \lvert A\rvert$, the phase is the straight line $-M\omega$ (plus $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ for the $j$), and it jumps by $\pi$ wherever $A$ changes sign. The [[demos/practice-drills|Magnitude & phase drill]] practises exactly this factoring.

## What the demo is (and isn't)

It handles **real** systems only (real taps, real coefficients, conjugate root pairs), because the output formula $\lvert H_d(\omega_0)\rvert\cos(\omega_0 n + \theta + \angle H_d(\omega_0))$ needs a real $h[n]$ ([[3-fourier-analysis/15-frequency-response|Lecture 15]]). The input cosine is applied for all $n$, so there is no start-up transient: the stems show the steady state.

The numbers are floating point:

- **Grid.** The curves use 2401 frequencies on $[-\pi, \pi]$, which includes every multiple of $\pi/24$. The readouts evaluate $H_d(\omega_0)$ directly. A value is shown as $\sqrt{19}$ or $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ when such a form matches it to $10^{-9}$; that is a display, not exact arithmetic.
- **Phase conventions.** The principal value lies in $[-\pi, \pi]$. A negative real $H_d$ is shown as $+\pi$ for $\omega \ge 0$ and $-\pi$ for $\omega < 0$, which keeps the phase of a real system odd, as in the course plots. Where $\lvert H_d\rvert$ is below $10^{-9}$ of its maximum the phase is undefined, and the curve has a gap. *Unwrap* applies `numpy.unwrap` outward from $\omega = 0$. It removes the $2\pi$ wraps, but the $\pi$ jumps at zeros on the unit circle stay, because those are real discontinuities of the phase.
- **Group delay** is computed from the formula $\tau(\omega) = n_0 + \operatorname{Re}\frac{\sum k b_k e^{-j\omega k}}{\sum b_k e^{-j\omega k}} - \operatorname{Re}\frac{\sum k a_k e^{-j\omega k}}{\sum a_k e^{-j\omega k}}$, not by differentiating the sampled phase, so a π jump never shows up as a spike.
- **Direct check.** For FIR systems the orange circles are the convolution sum $\sum_k h[k]x[n-k]$ evaluated with the eternal input. For IIR systems they come from running the causal recursion from far enough in the past that the transient has decayed below $10^{-13}$; a pole within $0.00075$ of the unit circle would need more than 40 000 samples for that, and then the check is left out. A causal system with a pole on or outside the unit circle gets no check, because its DTFT does not exist. For a pole outside, the curves show $H(z)$ on $\lvert z\rvert = 1$, the frequency response of the one stable (non-causal) system with that $H(z)$. For a pole on the circle no system with that $H(z)$ is stable, and the curves blow up at the pole's angle.

Every preset was checked against `scipy.signal.freqz` and `group_delay`, `numpy.angle` and `numpy.unwrap`, `numpy.roots`, and an independent `np.convolve` / `lfilter` simulation. The scripts are `verify/INT_fr_check.py` and `verify/INT_page_numbers.py` (every number on this page). The [[demos/python-demos|Python demos]] show how to do the same computations in a few lines.
